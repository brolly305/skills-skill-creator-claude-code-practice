A correction — six prompts rebuilt as a sequence

How To Actually
Learn Anything

The popular version assumes you've already picked a topic, have twenty clean hours, and will be honest about what you understood. Fix those three and the method changes shape entirely.

The original six, scored

Learn it in 20 hoursFix
One-page cheat sheetFix
Quiz me until I breakKeep
Learning ladderFix
Signal in the noiseCut
Feynman loopKeep

Both keeps are retrieval loops — you answer, you're found wanting, the gap closes. Both are also the uncomfortable ones, which predicts exactly which two get skipped. The cut is "match your learning style": that hypothesis has repeatedly failed to replicate.

What to do instead

Each card opens to the prompt to paste. Words in [brackets] are yours to fill in.

0

Decide, or admit you haven't

Before any plan exists
Missing from the original
  • What act will I be able to do that I can't now? Not "understand X" — ship X.
  • What forces the date? No forcing event means it slips. That's a prediction, not a scolding.
  • What am I dropping to make room? Time is conserved.
  • Can't choose between topics? Don't. Give each three hours, then notice which one you thought about afterward.

PreventsSix weeks of study that was really avoidance of a harder decision.

The prompts

Test the decision

I'm thinking about learning [topic]. Before you help me plan
anything, push back on the decision itself.

Ask me, one at a time:
1. What will I be able to DO afterward that I can't do now?
2. What deadline or event forces this?
3. What am I dropping to make room?

If my answers are vague, say so. Then tell me honestly whether
this looks like a learning project or just an interest — and
whether it might be a way of avoiding something harder.

When you can't choose

I can't decide between [A], [B] and [C]. Don't pick for me.

For each one, give me the smallest real task I could do in
about three hours that would tell me whether I actually want
to go deeper. Real output — not reading or watching.
1

Measure your starting point

Twenty minutes, saves hours
  • Make Claude ask you eight questions first — including about adjacent fields.
  • Then: what can I skip, and what will I think I know and actually don't?

PreventsBeing taught what you already half-know. Confident wrong knowledge you never look up.

The prompt

Diagnose me first

I want to be able to [specific act] by [date].

Before you plan anything: ask me eight questions that would
tell you what I already know, including from adjacent fields.
Ask them one at a time and don't soften your assessment.

Then tell me which parts of this topic I can skip because I
already have them, and which parts I'm likely to think I know
and actually don't.
2

Get a map, hold it loosely

The fixed 80/20
  • Ask for the 20% for your specific act — not the 20% of the field. Different lists.
  • Also ask: what's commonly taught that I don't need, where do practitioners disagree, where are you least confident.
  • Then check the map against one human or one primary source.

PreventsSpending ten sessions on a fluent map you had no standing to audit.

The prompt

The map, with its weak spots showing

Give me the smallest set of concepts I need to [specific act]
— not the 20% of the field in general, the 20% for that act.

Then three things:
1. What's commonly taught in this topic that I do NOT need
   for this outcome, and why it's usually included anyway.
2. Where practitioners genuinely disagree, so I know what
   isn't settled.
3. Where you are least confident, or where your information
   is most likely to be out of date.

Keep it to one screen.
3

Run the loop

Not 10 × 2 hours
  • 25–45 minutes. Short enough to survive a weeknight — so you'll actually do it.
  • Open cold. Five minutes writing what you remember, no notes. Uncomfortable, highest value.
  • Close with an artifact. Notes don't count — notes record having been present.
  • Return at day 1, 3, 7, 21. Spacing is what you still have next quarter.

PreventsA 20-hour sprint that's mostly gone in a month.

The prompts

The strict grader

Quiz me on [topic], ten questions, increasing difficulty.

Grade me strictly. You have a bias toward encouragement —
override it. If an answer is vague, hand-wavy, or uses a term
correctly without showing I understand it, mark it WRONG and
say exactly which part was empty.

After each answer: grade, name the specific gap, re-explain
only what I missed. Then ask a harder version of the same
question to check the repair held.

The Feynman loop

I'm going to explain [concept] in my own words. Don't correct
me as I go. When I'm done, tell me:
- which parts I got right
- which parts I got away with — used the right words without
  showing I understand them
- the one question that would expose my weakest spot

Then re-teach only what I got wrong, and have me explain it
again until the gaps are gone.
4

Make contact

By session three, ready or not
Missing from the original
  • Ship the smallest working version to someone who doesn't have to be nice about it.
  • Or teach one real person and watch where their face goes blank.
  • Do it badly and early. Premature contact is cheap; deferred contact is six wasted weeks.

PreventsSix blocks of generated text mistaken for ability.

The prompt

Design the first real test

I've done three sessions on [topic]. Design the smallest real
test of whether I can [specific act] — something I can do
this week, with a real person or under real conditions, where
failing is cheap.

Afterward I'll tell you what happened. Help me separate what it
revealed about my skill from what it revealed about the map
we've been working from.
5

Stop on purpose

Written at phase 0
Missing from the original
  • Done looks like: the act from phase 0, performed unaided. Then say so and stop.
  • Abandon if: the forcing event vanished, or three sessions produced nothing showable.
  • Both endings are wins. Quitting on a written condition is a decision; drifting is attrition with a story attached.

PreventsThe topic that sits on you, unfinished and unquit, for a year.

The prompt

The honest check-in

At the start I wrote:
  Done looks like: [the act]
  Abandon if: [the condition]

Where I actually am now: [honest status].

Tell me plainly which applies — done, abandon, or continue.
If it's continue, say specifically what's left. Don't soften
it, and don't talk me into continuing out of sunk cost.

The one rule underneath all six

Don't ask it to explain. Ask it to judge.

Asking Claude to explain produces fluent text, a feeling of comprehension, and almost no durable knowledge. It's the mode that feels best and does least.

Attempt first, then submit. Write your own cheat sheet, then ask what's missing. Explain it in your words, then ask what you got away with. Solve it, then ask for the case that breaks your solution.

And when you use it as a grader, tell it plainly: you have a bias toward encouragement — override it, and mark vague answers wrong. A lenient judge reopens the exact hole the exercise exists to close.

Decide the bad week now

  • Ten minutes still counts. Cold retrieval only, no new material.
  • A missed week isn't a failed project. Resume at your last artifact, never from the beginning.
  • Three empty sessions is data — a signal the forcing event went away, not a character flaw. Go back to phase 0.
On the sources: the twenty-hour figure comes from Josh Kaufman's The First 20 Hours (2013), where it describes reaching passable ability at a narrowly defined sub-skill — not a domain. The evidence against matching instruction to "learning styles" is summarized in Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008). Retrieval practice and spacing are among the better-replicated findings in learning research; the day 1 / 3 / 7 / 21 intervals here are a serviceable default rather than a precise result.
