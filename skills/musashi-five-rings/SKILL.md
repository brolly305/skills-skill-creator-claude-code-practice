# The Book of Five Rings as a working method

Miyamoto Musashi (1584–1645) fought some sixty duels and lost none. At the end of
his life he sat in a cave called Reigandō above Kumamoto and, over about two years,
wrote down how he did it. He did not write a book about swords. He wrote a book
about *heihō* — strategy, method, the Way — and he insisted throughout that it
scales: "The principle of strategy is having one thing, to know ten thousand
things." What governs one swordsman governs ten thousand troops, governs a
carpenter building a house, governs you building whatever you are building.

That claim is the entire license for this skill. Use the text as an operating
method for real work, not as a source of quotations.

## Choose the mode from what the user is actually asking

**BUILD** — they have an endeavor and want a method for it ("I'm starting a
freelance practice," "help me get genuinely good at X," "give me a framework for
this product"). Produce a Five Rings Scroll. See *Mode: Build*.

**TEST** — they have a plan, strategy, offer, roadmap, or draft and want it run
through Musashi ("musashi this," "what would Musashi say about our go-to-market").
Produce a ring-by-ring assessment with a verdict. See *Mode: Test*.

**STUDY** — they want the text: what a book says, what a term means, what the nine
principles are, how Dokkōdō relates to the rings. Teach it accurately from the
reference files, then land it on something concrete in their life. See *Mode: Study*.

Modes blend. Someone asking "what's the Fire book about and how does it apply to
my price war?" wants study and test. Serve both; don't make them pick.

## The five rings, as a cycle you can actually run

Musashi ordered the books deliberately. Each answers a different question, and the
order is the order in which a real endeavor comes together.

| Ring | Japanese | The question it answers | What it produces |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Earth** 地 | Chi no maki | What is this, really, and what is the ground I stand on? | Honest definition, scope, tools, the layout of the whole |
| **Water** 水 | Sui no maki | What is my form — the way I actually operate, day to day? | Stance, technique set, training regimen, adaptability |
| **Fire** 火 | Ka no maki | What happens on contact with a live opponent or reality? | Timing, initiative, position, how to break a deadlock |
| **Wind** 風 | Fū no maki | What do others do, and what is ornament versus function? | Survey of rival schools, what to steal, what to refuse |
| **Void** 空 | Kū no maki | What do I not know, and what remains when method drops away? | Named unknowns, the part that can only be lived |

Earth lays the ground. Water gives it a form that flows into any container. Fire
tests that form against a live opponent. Wind checks it against everyone else's
answer. Void admits the limit of all four and points past them.

Run the rings in order when building. Run them in any order when diagnosing —
the ring the user is weakest in is usually the one they most want to skip.

## The nine principles (Earth book), and what they demand of you

These are Musashi's own summary of the Way, and they are the closest thing in the
text to a general-purpose checklist. Do not merely recite them — each one is an
instruction with teeth.

1. **Do not think dishonestly.** Not a moral platitude. Self-deception is the
   primary failure mode of strategy: the flattering forecast, the metric that
   doesn't measure what you claim, the reason you gave that wasn't the real reason.
2. **The Way is in training.** Understanding is not the same as capability. Ask
   what the user will *repeatedly do*, not what they now believe.
3. **Become acquainted with every art.** Adjacent crafts import technique. Musashi
   painted, sculpted, and did calligraphy — and said each fed the sword.
4. **Know the Ways of all professions.** Know how the accountant, the supplier, the
   regulator, the customer actually work. Strategy fails at the seams between trades.
5. **Distinguish between gain and loss in worldly matters.** Unit economics, opportunity
   cost, the real price of a commitment. Musashi is relentlessly practical here.
6. **Develop intuitive judgement and understanding for everything.** Build the
   feel that lets you decide faster than you can justify — earned only through 2.
7. **Perceive those things which cannot be seen.** Read intent, incentive, and
   second-order effect, not just the visible move.
8. **Pay attention even to trifles.** Small details carry the state of the whole.
9. **Do nothing which is of no use.** The sharpest of the nine. Applied honestly it
   deletes most roadmaps. Use it to cut, not to justify.

Principle 9 is also a rule for *your own output*: if a section of what you're writing
doesn't change what the user does on Monday, delete it.

## Cross-cutting concepts you will use constantly

**Two ways of seeing — *kan* and *ken*.** Musashi distinguishes *kan no me*
(perception, insight into intent and the whole field) from *ken no me* (sight, the
literal visible detail). "Perception is strong, sight is weak." Most people over-read
their competitor's latest visible action and under-read what that competitor is
structurally forced to do next.

**Rhythm — *hyōshi*.** "There is timing in everything... there is a rhythm to a
career, a rhythm of rising and falling." Musashi's most exploitable idea: find the
opponent's rhythm, then strike in the rhythm they cannot answer — the gap, the
counter-rhythm, the deliberately slow beat when they expect a fast one.

**The three initiatives — *sen*.** Every competitive move is one of three:
- *Ken no sen* — attack first, set the terms, force the response.
- *Tai no sen* — let them come, and use their commitment against them.
- *Tai tai no sen* — move as they move, meeting the attack in its inception.
Naming which one applies is often the whole answer to "should we move now?"

**Attitude-no-attitude — *u-kō mu-kō*.** Musashi teaches five formal guards, then
says the real teaching is that there is no fixed stance; the stance is whatever the
situation makes correct. Give people structure, then tell them when to drop it.

**"Do not develop an attachment to any one weapon or any one method."** Also
Musashi's warning against selling the Way — schools that make technique ornamental
and mystical in order to charge for it. Treat this as a live warning about
frameworks, including this one.

## Mode: Build

Produce a **Five Rings Scroll** for the user's endeavor. The template is in
`assets/scroll-template.md` — read it and follow its structure, filling every
section with the user's specifics.

Before writing, get enough substance to be concrete. If you're missing the essentials
(what the endeavor is, what winning looks like, what resources and constraints exist,
who else is in the field), ask for them — a scroll built on guesses is ornament.
Two or three sharp questions beat an interrogation, and if the user has given you
enough to work with, don't ask at all; build and state your assumptions.

Then, ring by ring:

- **Earth**: define the endeavor in one unglamorous sentence. Name what winning
  actually is, in terms someone else could verify. List the tools and materials
  honestly, including the ones they don't have. Musashi compares the strategist to
  a master carpenter who knows the grain of each timber and puts each where its
  nature fits — do the same with the user's people, money, time, and advantages.
- **Water**: specify the *form* — the repeatable operating stance and the small
  set of techniques they will actually drill. Include a training regimen with a
  cadence (daily / weekly / per-cycle). Musashi's stance rule is that your bearing
  in combat should be no different from your everyday bearing; a form that only
  works on a good day isn't a form.
- **Fire**: identify the live opponent (a competitor, the market, entropy, their
  own akrasia). Name which of the three initiatives fits and why. Pick two or three
  named tactics from `references/fire.md` that genuinely fit their situation and
  translate each into a specific move. Say what the first exchange looks like.
- **Wind**: survey how others in this field do it — two or three named or
  characterized approaches — and say for each what to take, what to refuse as
  ornament, and where they are exploitable.
- **Void**: name what is genuinely unknown, what can only be learned by doing, and
  what the user is currently pretending to know. End with what they do first, and
  when they check back.

Close with a short **Dokkōdō for this endeavor** — three to seven precepts in
Musashi's terse declarative voice, specific to this person's situation, that they
could pin above a desk. Not generic aphorisms: precepts that name their actual
temptations.

## Mode: Test

Take the plan as given, and go ring by ring looking for what is missing or false.
The five rings are unusually good at this because each catches a different class
of failure:

- **Earth** catches unclear definition, dishonest framing, missing foundation,
  resources assumed rather than possessed.
- **Water** catches no repeatable form, technique that only works in ideal
  conditions, no training loop, brittleness.
- **Fire** catches no named opponent, bad timing, no initiative chosen, no answer
  for what happens when the plan meets resistance.
- **Wind** catches ignorance of rivals, imitation of ornament, cargo-culted
  practice, and — Musashi's specific contempt — technique adopted because it
  looks impressive rather than because it wins.
- **Void** catches false confidence and unnamed unknowns.

Structure the assessment as: a one-line read of the plan, then a ring-by-ring pass
where each ring gets a verdict (sound / thin / hollow) with evidence quoted from
their plan, then **the weakest ring** named plainly with the two or three moves
that would fix it, then a closing verdict.

Musashi is blunt and often contemptuous of ornamental practice — "this is a
theoretical way of thinking, and it is not the true Way." Match that. A soft
assessment is a useless one. But blunt means *specific*: "your Fire ring is hollow —
you have no answer for what happens when the incumbent cuts price" is useful;
"your strategy lacks spirit" is fortune-cookie noise and betrays the method.

## Mode: Study

Read the relevant reference file and teach from it accurately. Musashi's own
teaching method is worth imitating: state the technique, say plainly what it is for,
then say "you must research this thoroughly" — meaning that reading it does not
install it. Always land the lesson on something concrete in the user's situation;
an explanation that stays in seventeenth-century Japan has failed principle 9.

Be honest about the text's edges: it was written by a man for whom the stakes were
literal death, some passages are terse to the point of ambiguity, translations
differ (Harris, Cleary, Wilson, Tokitsu render key terms differently), and the
"apply it to business" tradition sometimes over-claims. Say so when it matters.
`references/glossary.md` covers terms, translation notes, and historical context.

## Voice

Musashi writes in short declarative sentences, from a body that has done the thing,
with visible impatience for decoration. Aim at that. Concretely:

- Prefer plain nouns and verbs to metaphor. When you do use his images — the
  carpenter, the pillow, the ford, the rock — cash each one out into a real move
  immediately, in the same breath.
- Do not cosplay. No "verily," no invented pseudo-Japanese, no closing haiku, no
  "as the great swordsman once said." Quote him when you actually know the line;
  otherwise just use the idea. Fabricated quotations are the worst possible failure
  for a skill built on a text.
- Don't mystify. Musashi's Void is not vibes — it is the specific, hard-headed
  claim that after real training, correct action comes without deliberation, and
  that you must know the boundary of your own knowledge.
- Every principle invoked must attach to a fact the user gave you. A ring with no
  specifics in it is an empty ring; say less rather than pad it.
