# Book of Wind (風の巻, Fū no maki) — the other schools

The character 風 means both "wind" and, in this usage, **style or tradition** —
"the wind of other houses," their manner and custom. This book is Musashi's survey
of rival schools of swordsmanship and what he thinks is wrong with each.

His stated reason for including it is the thing that makes it useful: **"It is
difficult to realize the true Way just through sword-fencing. Know the smallest
things and the biggest things, the shallowest and the deepest. As if it were a
straight road mapped out on the ground..."** — you cannot know your own approach
without knowing the alternatives. The Wind book is competitive analysis as an
epistemic requirement, not as an optional extra.

Use this book when the work is about **rivals, competing methodologies, industry
convention, best practices, and telling function from ornament**.

## The critiques, and what each one is really about

Musashi goes school by school. Each critique generalizes cleanly.

**Schools using an extra-long sword.** Some schools prefer the longest blade,
believing reach wins. Musashi says this comes from weakness — they want to win
from a distance where they feel safe, and reach is useless in a confined room, or
in a crowd, or when the enemy is already inside it. The long sword also cannot be
freely wielded and becomes a burden.
*Generalizes to*: advantages that only function at a distance the opponent controls.
Scale, budget, and brand reach are real, but a strategy that depends on never being
approached closely is a strategy with a fixed weakness. And the preference for
range is often fear wearing the clothes of tactics.

**Schools of the strong sword.** Some emphasize a powerful, forceful cut. Musashi:
if you try to cut strongly, the cut is *worse*, because force introduces
wildness — and one cannot cut a man in two with brute force alone. Strength applied
crudely at the moment of contact is a liability; the cut needs to be *sufficient*,
not maximal. There is no such thing as a "weak" or "strong" long sword, only a
correct one.
*Generalizes to*: overwhelming force as a substitute for accuracy — the ad blitz,
the all-hands push, the ten-person team on a two-person problem. Effort is not a
strategy, and excess effort degrades precision.

**Schools using the shorter sword.** Others go the opposite way: a shorter blade
for speed, to slip inside the enemy's swing and take the gaps. Musashi calls this
defensive in spirit — waiting for the opponent's openings rather than *making* the
situation. It works against one careless opponent, fails against many, and produces
a fighter who is always reacting.
*Generalizes to*: agility as an identity. Being fast and opportunistic is real, but
a method that consists entirely of exploiting others' mistakes has no answer when
the field is competent, and it never sets terms.

**Schools with many techniques.** Some schools teach vast catalogues of named
techniques — Musashi says this is partly for the market: an impressive-looking
curriculum to sell to beginners. His objection is that the enemy is cut in a small
number of ways and the rest is decoration; a large repertoire cannot be drilled
deeply, and technique choice under pressure should not require a menu.
*Generalizes to*: the framework collection, the fifty-tool marketing stack, the
certification ladder. Ask what fraction is ever actually used, and whether the
breadth is for the practitioner or for the brochure.

**Fixing of the attitudes (rigid stances).** Schools that treat guards as the
substance of the art have it backwards; Musashi has already taught
attitude-no-attitude. A fixed stance is appropriate to a world where nothing moves,
and there is no such world. He notes that rigid formality belongs to an era of
fixed rules and set-piece contests, not to actual fighting.
*Generalizes to*: process worship. The org that defends the ritual when the ritual
has stopped producing.

**Fixing the eyes.** Some schools fix the gaze on the enemy's sword, or hands, or
face, or feet. Musashi says fixing on any specific thing is how you get beaten —
you become "bewitched" and forget the strategy. Perception (*kan*) over sight
(*ken*); see the whole.
*Generalizes to*: the single metric, the dashboard everyone stares at, the one
competitor who occupies all the attention. Whatever you fix your eyes on is the
thing your rival can use as bait.

**Use of the feet (fancy footwork).** Schools teach floating steps, springing
steps, jumping steps, treading steps, crow's-foot steps. Musashi objects on
practical grounds: fields are muddy, roads are stony, paths are narrow, and in many
real places you cannot jump or spring at all. Walk normally.
*Generalizes to*: methods that require ideal conditions. If the process only works
on a clean codebase, a full team, and a calm quarter, it is a jumping step.

**Speed in other schools.** Some make speed the point. Musashi: **true strategy has
no dependence on speed.** The expert never appears fast, because they are on the
rhythm; hurrying is a sign of being *off* it. He compares it to a skilled traveler
who covers more ground without seeming to rush, and to musicians — an expert singer
or dancer performs at the correct tempo, and a beginner who rushes appears busy and
achieves less.
*Generalizes to*: the velocity cult. Shipping speed measured as a virtue in itself,
sprints that consume more than they produce. The question is never "how fast" but
"in time with what."

**"Interior" and "surface" — secret teachings.** Many schools have esoteric inner
transmissions revealed only to advanced students. Musashi rejects this entirely: in
strategy there is no surface and no interior, no secret and no gate. He teaches
according to what the student can absorb, and gradually raises the level, but there
is nothing withheld as mystery — "it is not possible to write down the secret of
this Way and then keep it hidden."
*Generalizes to*: the paywalled inner circle, the mastermind, the methodology whose
core is only available at the higher tier. Musashi's charge is that mystique exists
to create dependence and to charge for it, and that it is evidence against the
method rather than for it.

## The charge underneath all of them: selling the Way

Musashi returns repeatedly to one accusation: that these schools have made
swordsmanship into a **commercial and ornamental art**. They "sell the Way,"
teaching what looks impressive, "colouring and decorating" technique to attract
students, making a commodity of themselves. He calls this the sin of turning a
thing that must work into a thing that must sell.

This is the sharpest tool the Wind book gives you, and it should be aimed
symmetrically — including at the Five Rings industry itself, and at this skill.
The test he implies: **would this technique still be taught if no one were watching
and no one were paying?** If a practice exists mainly to be seen — the impressive
deck, the elaborate process, the framework named after its author — treat it as
decoration until proven otherwise.

## Using the Wind method on a modern field

Musashi's own procedure is worth copying directly. For each rival approach:

1. **State what they actually do**, accurately and without straw-manning. His
   critiques are specific about technique, which is why they land.
2. **Identify the belief underneath it** — what they think wins. (Reach wins. Force
   wins. Speed wins. Breadth wins.)
3. **Find the conditions where that belief fails** — the confined room, the muddy
   field, the many opponents. Every school's strength names its own terrain.
4. **Say what to take.** Musashi is not purely dismissive: he adopts what works and
   his objection is to *fixation*, not to the technique. A survey that finds nothing
   worth stealing is a survey done badly.
5. **Say what to refuse as ornament**, and why — mistaking decoration for function
   is the specific failure this book exists to prevent.

Do this for two or three rivals in the user's field, characterized concretely. A
Wind section that says "competitors are doing content marketing" has done none of
the five steps.

## How to use Wind in a build or a critique

**Building**: survey two or three named or characterized approaches in the user's
field. For each: what they do, what they believe wins, where that fails, what to
take, what to refuse. Then state plainly what the user's approach is that these
others are not — and if the answer is "nothing," that is the most valuable finding
in the scroll.

**Critiquing**: Wind is thin when the plan shows no knowledge of how others solve
this; when it copies an incumbent's practices without asking which of those
practices are load-bearing for a company of that size and which are decoration;
when it treats an industry convention as a law of nature; when it is differentiated
only by intensity ("we'll do the same thing but better/harder"); or when the
strategy is built around something that mainly looks impressive.
