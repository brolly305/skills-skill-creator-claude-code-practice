# Book of Water (水の巻, Sui no maki) — form

"Water adopts the shape of its receptacle; it is sometimes a trickle and sometimes
a wild sea." Musashi names this book for water because it is about **form that
holds under any container** — your stance, your gaze, your footwork, the small set
of techniques you actually drill, and the state of mind you carry into all of them.

Use this book when the work is about **how the user operates day to day**: the
repeatable practice, the training regimen, the default posture, adaptability, and
what to do when conditions change but the work still has to happen.

## Spiritual bearing

Musashi's first instruction on form is about mental state, and it is deliberately
anticlimactic: **in strategy your spiritual bearing must not be any different from
normal.** Both in fighting and in everyday life you should be determined but calm;
meet the situation without tenseness, yet not recklessly; the spirit settled but
unbiased; calm in the body and unmoved in the mind, or lively in the body and
unmoved in the mind.

Do not let the body be led by the spirit, or the spirit by the body. Do not let the
enemy see your spirit. Small people must know the spirit of large people, and large
people the spirit of small.

The usable claim: **a form that requires an exceptional state to execute is not a
form.** If a working method only functions on a good day, when motivation is high,
when the quarter is calm, it will not be there when it matters. This is the standard
against which any proposed routine, ritual, or process should be checked.

## Stance and gaze

**Stance**: hold the head level, neither drooping nor tilted; the gaze steady;
forehead unwrinkled; shoulders down; back straight; the whole body settled so that
"your combat stance is your everyday stance and your everyday stance is your combat
stance." That last line is the point of the entire section.

**Gaze** — *kan* and *ken*: Musashi distinguishes two ways of seeing.
- *Ken no me* — **sight**: the literal, visible, near detail. The opponent's sword.
- *Kan no me* — **perception**: the wider read — intent, distribution, the whole
  field, what is about to happen.

"Perception is strong and sight weak." Look widely, take in the situation without
moving the eyeballs, see both sides without turning the head. In a large battle
this becomes: observe the enemy's *strength* — numbers, condition, morale,
disposition — not just the enemy's *position*.

The practical instruction: it is difficult to perceive what is close, so train the
long view; and know your own weapon and hands without looking at them, so that
attention is freed for the field. Modern translation: automate and internalize the
near work so your attention can live at the level where the decisions are.

## Footwork

Move with the tips of the toes slightly floating and the heel pressed down firmly.
Musashi names three faults: the **jumping foot**, the **floating foot**, and the
**stamping foot** — and adds "treading foot" in some renderings. All are ways of
moving that commit you before the situation resolves.

The correct footwork uses *yin-yang* — never move only one foot. Step
right-left-right-left, cutting, withdrawing, parrying. The rule is that **movement
should always be paired**, never a single committed lunge that leaves you with no
next step.

Applied: any operating rhythm where one move leaves you unable to make the next
one is a jumping foot. Cash-flow commitments, hiring, publishing cadence — the
question is always whether the step you're taking leaves the following step available.

## The five attitudes, and attitude-no-attitude

Musashi teaches five guards (*kamae*):
- **Upper** (jōdan)
- **Middle** (chūdan)
- **Lower** (gedan)
- **Left side** (hidari waki)
- **Right side** (migi waki)

He calls the middle attitude the seat of them all — "the origin of strategy." He
also says the upper and lower attitudes are convertible into the middle depending
on the place, and that the side attitudes exist for situations where the space
above or to one side is obstructed.

Then he undercuts the whole taxonomy with the teaching of **attitude-no-attitude**
(*u-kō mu-kō no oshie*, "existing attitude, non-existing attitude"): there are
attitudes, and there is no fixed attitude. You take a guard, but the guard is only
correct with reference to where you are and what the enemy is doing. The purpose of
the guard is to cut the enemy — "you must not be too concerned with the attitude
itself."

This is the single most transferable idea in the Water book. **Give people
structure, then tell them the conditions under which to abandon it.** A framework
taught without its own exit conditions produces people who defend the framework
instead of winning.

## The named techniques

Musashi's technique list is not a menu to recite; it is a demonstration that a
small vocabulary, deeply drilled, covers the field. The most useful for analogy:

- **In one timing** (*hitotsu-byōshi*) — strike in the single beat before the
  opponent has decided anything, while their mind is still unformed.
- **The abdomen timing of two** (*nikon no hyōshi*) — when the enemy braces as you
  attack, feint the attack, let them relax, then strike. Two beats, the second real.
- **No design, no conception** (*munen musō*) — when both are attacking, strike
  from the body and the sword with no deliberation at all. Available only after
  long training; this is the Water book's link to the Void.
- **Flowing water cut** (*ryūsui no uchi*) — when in a deadlock at close quarters,
  strike slowly and largely, like stagnant water flowing. Deliberate slowness as a
  weapon against someone braced for speed.
- **Continuous cut** (*renzoku / enren*) — when your attack is parried, continue in
  one motion into the next cut without resetting.
- **Fire and stones cut** (*sekka no atari*) — strike strongly from a position with
  no wind-up at all, moving hands, body, and legs together. Power without telegraph.
- **Red leaves cut** (*kōyō no uchi*) — strike specifically at the opponent's sword
  to knock it down: attack the instrument rather than the person. In modern terms,
  attack the mechanism the rival depends on, not their output.
- **The body in place of the long sword** — lead with the body, let the weapon
  follow. Committing your presence first, technique second.
- **Cut and slash** — Musashi insists these are different: the cut is decisive and
  committed, the slash is contact without resolution. "Even if you slash strongly...
  it makes little difference." Naming which one you are doing is half of honesty
  about a competitive move.
- **To stab at the face / at the heart** — precision applied to the point that
  makes the opponent flinch or that ends it, chosen deliberately.
- **The parry that is also a strike** (three ways to parry, the smacking parry) —
  defense that carries the counter inside it, so no beat is spent purely defending.

## Body techniques

- **Chinese monkey's body** (*shikkō no mi*) — do not stretch out the arms; move
  the whole body in. Reaching is how you get pulled off balance.
- **Glue and lacquer emulsion body** (*shikkitsu no mi*) — stick to the enemy,
  head, body, and legs together, with no gap for them to escape into.
- **To strive for height** (*take kurabe*) — when closing, stretch up and be taller
  than the enemy, meeting them head-on with no shrinking.
- **To apply stickiness** (*nebari*) — when swords cross, join and stay joined
  without force; different from entanglement, which is heavier and worse.
- **The body strike** (*tai atari*) — hit with the body itself, shoulder to chest,
  entering with a bounding spirit.
- **The body of a rock** — see `fire.md`; Musashi lists it there as the final teaching.

## Training

The Water book closes with the instruction that these techniques cannot be learned
from the page: "you cannot understand this book by merely looking at it." He
repeatedly says **you must research this thoroughly**, and means: practice until it
is in the body, then test it against a live opponent.

He also gives the scaling claim in its clearest form: what is written for one man
with a sword applies to ten thousand — "if you know the Way broadly you will see it
in everything." The instruction is *ichi ji banji* in spirit: from one thing, know
ten thousand things — train one thing deeply enough and it generalizes.

## How to use Water in a build or a critique

**Building**: specify the small set of techniques the user will actually drill and
the cadence at which they'll drill them (daily, weekly, per cycle). Define the
everyday stance — the default posture that must survive a bad week. Name where the
form flexes and where it holds. Include an explicit "attitude-no-attitude" clause:
under what conditions do they abandon the plan?

**Critiquing**: Water is thin when the plan has no repeatable operating form, only
goals; when the routine assumes ideal conditions (a jumping foot); when there is no
training loop, only knowledge acquisition; when every technique is defensive and
none carries a counter; when the person's attention lives on near detail (*ken*)
because the near work was never internalized; or when they're so attached to their
stance that they'd hold it while losing.
