# Book of the Void (空の巻, Kū no maki) — and the Dokkōdō

The shortest book — a page or two — and the one most often mangled into mysticism.
It is not mystical. It is Musashi's account of what remains when method has been
fully absorbed, and of the difference between genuine emptiness and mere ignorance.

Use this book when the work is about **the limits of knowledge, false confidence,
what can only be learned by doing, and what to do when there is no rule**.

## What Musashi actually says

The core statements, closely rendered:

- "What is called the spirit of the void is where there is nothing. It is not
  included in man's knowledge."
- **"By knowing things that exist, you can know that which does not exist. That is
  the void."** This is the load-bearing sentence. Emptiness is defined *negatively,
  from the edge of what you actually know*. It is reached by knowing a great deal,
  not by knowing nothing.
- People wrongly take the void to be what they cannot understand or distinguish —
  "this is not the true void. It is bewilderment."
- The warrior must "learn strategy thoroughly, practise diligently, and become
  attuned to the Way," until acting with a clear, undeluded mind, "with the true
  void as the Way" and the Way as the void.
- "In the void is virtue, and no evil. Wisdom has existence, principle has
  existence, the Way has existence, spirit is nothingness."

And in the Water book, the technique he calls **munen musō** — "no design, no
conception" — is the same idea in operational form: at the decisive moment, the
strike comes from the body without deliberation. Not because thinking is bad, but
because the thinking has already been done, thousands of times, in training.

## Void is not the same as not knowing

This distinction is the single most important thing to preserve when applying the
book, because the entire self-help tradition around Musashi collapses it.

| | Bewilderment | The void |
|---|---|---|
| Where it comes from | Never having done the work | Having done the work to its edge |
| What it feels like | Vague, comfortable, permissive | Precise, uncomfortable, specific |
| What it produces | "Trust your gut" | Action faster than justification |
| How it's tested | It isn't | Against a live opponent, repeatedly |

The practical consequence for anyone using this skill: **you may only invoke the
void about things the user has actually trained.** A beginner's intuition is not
emptiness; it is inexperience with better branding. For a beginner, the correct
Void section names the unknowns honestly and says what training would resolve
each — that *is* the void properly used, because it is knowing the boundary of
what you know.

## Using Void without going vague

The Void section of any output should be the most concrete part, not the least.
Three things belong in it:

1. **What is genuinely unknown** — named, not gestured at. "We don't know whether
   buyers in this segment will pay annually" is a void. "The future is uncertain"
   is noise.
2. **What can only be learned by doing** — the part where more analysis has zero
   marginal value and the only remaining instrument is contact with reality. Say
   what the first contact is and what it will teach.
3. **What the user is currently pretending to know** — the estimate presented as a
   fact, the assumption inherited from a blog post, the number that came from a
   feeling. This follows directly from principle 1, *do not think dishonestly*, and
   it is usually the most valuable sentence you will write.

A Void section that ends in serenity has failed. One that ends in a specific test
scheduled for a specific week has understood the book.

## The Dokkōdō — the way of walking alone

Musashi wrote the *Dokkōdō* (独行道) about a week before his death in 1645, and gave
it to his student Terao Magonojō along with the Five Rings. Twenty-one precepts,
each a single line. They are personal — a discipline he set for himself — and they
are notably harsher than anything in the Five Rings.

1. Accept everything just the way it is.
2. Do not seek pleasure for its own sake.
3. Do not, under any circumstances, depend on a partial feeling.
4. Think lightly of yourself and deeply of the world.
5. Be detached from desire your whole life long.
6. Do not regret what you have done.
7. Never be jealous.
8. Never let yourself be saddened by a separation.
9. Resentment and complaint are appropriate neither for oneself nor others.
10. Do not let yourself be guided by the feeling of lust or love.
11. In all things have no preferences.
12. Be indifferent to where you live.
13. Do not pursue the taste of good food.
14. Do not hold on to possessions you no longer need.
15. Do not act following customary beliefs.
16. Do not collect weapons or practise with weapons beyond what is useful.
17. Do not fear death.
18. Do not seek to possess either goods or fiefs for your old age.
19. Respect Buddha and the gods without counting on their help.
20. You may abandon your own body, but you must preserve your honour.
21. Never stray from the Way.

**How they relate to the rings.** The Five Rings is a method for winning; the
Dokkōdō is the character required to keep practising it without being deformed by
it. Several precepts map directly onto principles from the Earth book — 15 is
principle 1 (do not think dishonestly) turned outward at convention; 16 is
principle 9 (do nothing of no use) applied to acquisition; 14 is the same.

**Be honest about them.** This is a renunciate's list written by a man dying alone
who had given up family, home, and comfort for a discipline. Precepts 8, 10, and 12
are, taken literally, instructions to be unattached to people and places. Presented
as life advice to someone with a family and a mortgage, that is bad advice, and
pretending otherwise is a form of the flattery Musashi despised. The usable core is
narrower and still powerful: **do not let preference, resentment, comfort,
convention, or accumulation make your decisions for you.** Say this plainly if a
user takes the list as a program.

## Writing a Dokkōdō for a specific endeavor

A short set of precepts written in Musashi's voice, tuned to one person's actual
situation, is one of the most useful things this skill produces — it survives
after the analysis is forgotten. Rules for writing them:

- **Three to seven.** Twenty-one is his; yours should be cuttable to a sticky note.
- **Terse, declarative, second person or imperative.** No hedging, no "consider."
- **Name their actual temptation**, not a generic vice. "Do not take a client
  under £4k because the month looks empty" is a precept. "Be disciplined" is not.
- **Each should be checkable.** They should be able to tell at the end of a week
  whether they broke one.
- **At least one should hurt** — a precept that forbids the thing they most like
  doing. If the whole list is comfortable, you have written a mission statement.

Example of the register, for a freelancer:

> 1. Do not quote a price you have not decided before the call.
> 2. Take no work you would be embarrassed to show in a year.
> 3. Do not answer a brief you cannot restate in one sentence.
> 4. When the month is empty, sharpen the tools. Do not lower the price.
> 5. Never explain your rate twice.

## How to use Void in a build or a critique

**Building**: name the real unknowns, say which can only be resolved by contact,
call out what the user is pretending to know, state the first thing they do and
when they check back, and close with the endeavor's Dokkōdō.

**Critiquing**: Void is hollow when the plan has no acknowledged unknowns; when
uncertainty is mentioned only in a risks table nobody will reread; when confidence
is highest exactly where evidence is weakest; when the response to not knowing is
more planning rather than a cheap test; or when someone invokes intuition about a
domain in which they have no training — bewilderment presented as emptiness.
