# Book of Earth (地の巻, Chi no maki) — the ground

Musashi calls this book "Ground" or "Earth" because it is the level, surveyed
ground on which everything else stands. It is where he defines what strategy *is*,
who it's for, and how to lay out a whole endeavor before touching technique.

Use this book when the work at hand is **definition, scope, foundation, allocation
of people and materials, and choice of tools** — the parts of a project that get
skipped because they feel like preamble.

## What Musashi says he is doing

He opens with credentials, stated flatly: he fought his first duel at thirteen
against Arima Kihei, another at sixteen, and by the time he was about twenty-nine
he had fought more than sixty contests and never lost.

Then comes the most important sentence in the book for anyone using it as a method.
Having won sixty times, he says that when he reached thirty he looked back and
realized he had *not* won because of mastery of strategy — perhaps he had natural
ability, or the other schools were simply weaker. So he trained morning and
evening seeking the underlying principle, and only at about fifty did he feel he
had arrived at the Way.

Two things follow from this, and they should shape any use of the text:

1. **Winning is not proof of method.** A record of success can come from talent,
   from weak opposition, or from luck. Musashi audits his own wins for causation.
   Anyone presenting a track record as evidence of a Way should face the same audit.
2. **He claims no teacher and no borrowed authority.** He states that he writes
   without relying on Buddhist or Confucian doctrine, old war chronicles, or
   traditional commentary — "I take up my brush... using the Way of heaven and
   Kwannon as a mirror." The method is meant to stand on whether it works.

He wrote it in a cave called Reigandō, on Mount Iwato near Kumamoto, beginning
around 1643 and finishing shortly before his death in 1645.

## Strategy is a trade, not a mystique

Musashi addresses the common formula that the Way of the warrior is the resolute
acceptance of death — and rejects it as a *distinguishing* feature. Priests, women,
and peasants also face death without flinching, he notes. What distinguishes the
warrior is competence at winning: winning the duel, winning for one's lord,
winning renown. Strategy is the trade by which that is done.

This matters when applying the text. Musashi is not selling nobility, sacrifice,
or aesthetic suffering. He is selling *efficacy*. Any reading of the Five Rings
that ends in beautiful resignation rather than a better move has inverted him.

He also frames the four Ways of the world — the gentleman warrior, the farmer, the
artisan, the merchant — and deliberately chooses the **artisan's** Way as his
analogy for strategy, because the artisan works with tools, materials, plans, and
crews, and is judged by whether the thing stands up.

## The carpenter analogy

This is the longest sustained metaphor in the book, and the most directly usable.

**The foreman carpenter** knows the architectural rules of the whole country, the
proportions of buildings, the customs of the region. He knows the plans. He directs
the crew. Musashi's point: the commander must know the *whole layout* before
allocating anyone to anything.

**Knowing the timber** is the heart of it. The foreman selects wood by nature and
places each where its nature fits:
- Straight-grained, unblemished, good-looking timber → the front pillars, what is seen.
- Strong but slightly flawed timber → the inner pillars, where strength matters and looks don't.
- Fine-appearing but weak timber → thresholds, lintels, doors, sliding screens.
- Knotted, crooked, weak wood → scaffolding, and later firewood.

The lesson is not "use good materials." It is that **there is no such thing as bad
timber, only timber in the wrong place** — and that the foreman's real skill is
seeing the grain of what he actually has, not wishing for better stock. Applied to
people: the difficult, spiky specialist is an inner pillar, not a front pillar.
Applied to features, channels, or assets: the same.

**Allocating the crew** follows: the foreman knows who can lay floors, who makes
doors and screens, who can only carry. He assigns by ability and checks the work.
He knows how fast each man works, watches their spirit, and does not demand what
cannot be done.

**Tools**: the carpenter sharpens his own tools, carries them in his box, uses the
plane and the square and the measuring line skilfully. He builds so that the work
does not warp, so joints meet, so it isn't merely finished but *true*.

**Efficiency**: Musashi tells the carpenter to know when to work quickly and when
slowly, to grade the work by importance, and to not waste effort on what will
never be seen or bear load. This is principle 9 in physical form.

## Two swords: Niten Ichi-ryū

Musashi's school is Niten Ichi-ryū — "two heavens as one" — and the two-sword
method is the emblem of his approach.

His argument is practical, not symbolic. A warrior carries two swords; it is
wasteful to die with one still in the belt. He acknowledges the difficulty: the
long sword is harder to wield one-handed. So the *training* purpose of holding one
in each hand is to build the strength and control to use the long sword in one
hand — because in real conditions you may have the other hand on the reins, or be
running through a marsh or a crowd, or be carrying something.

The generalizable idea: **train in a harder configuration than the one you expect
to fight in, and never let a resource sit idle because convention says it's
secondary.** Also — Musashi is candid that the two-sword style feels awkward at
first and that difficulty at the start is the normal price of any Way.

## Do not have a favorite weapon

Musashi surveys the arsenal with unsentimental judgment: the spear and halberd
for the open field (the spear better for taking the initiative, the halberd more
defensive); the short sword for confined spaces; the bow good in the opening
exchanges of a battle and weak in a siege; the gun unmatched before the lines
close, and useless after; horses that must have stamina and no weak habits.

Then the rule: **do not develop an attachment to any one weapon, or become
over-familiar with it more than any other.** Over-attachment to a weapon is a fault
in the same class as ignorance of it.

Modern reading: this is the strongest line in the text against the one-tactic
operator — the person whose answer to every problem is the channel, language,
framework, or move they already like. It applies to methodologies too, including
this one.

## Timing in strategy

"There is timing in everything." Musashi says timing governs dancing and music
(where it is easy to see), the whole life of a warrior in thriving and declining,
harmony and discord, and the Way of the merchant in the rise and fall of capital.

He distinguishes several kinds, and the distinctions are the useful part:
- The timing that **matches** and the timing that **does not match** — moves that
  land in the rhythm, and moves that fall between beats.
- **Background timing** (*haikei no hyōshi*) — the larger rhythm of the situation
  underneath the exchange: the season, the funding cycle, the news cycle, the
  opponent's fatigue.
- The timing of **distance**, and the timing **of the void** — the gap, the beat
  the opponent is not occupying.

The strategic instruction is: read the opponent's rhythm, then move in a rhythm
they cannot answer — including the *slow* rhythm when they are braced for speed.
"From among all these you must beat the enemy with the timing of the void." See
`fire.md` for the tactics that operationalize this.

## The nine principles

Musashi's own summary of the Way of strategy, given at the end of the Earth book
as the rules for learning it:

1. **Do not think dishonestly.**
2. **The Way is in training.**
3. **Become acquainted with every art.**
4. **Know the Ways of all professions.**
5. **Distinguish between gain and loss in worldly matters.**
6. **Develop intuitive judgement and understanding for everything.**
7. **Perceive those things which cannot be seen.**
8. **Pay attention even to trifles.**
9. **Do nothing which is of no use.**

Notes for applying them, since bare recitation is worthless:

- **1** is epistemic before it is ethical. The dishonest thought is usually the
  comfortable one, and it is usually about your own position. Ask what the user
  would have to admit for their plan to be wrong.
- **2** is the hinge between knowing and being able. When someone asks how to get
  good at something, principle 2 says the answer is a schedule, not a reading list.
  Musashi ends nearly every technical passage with "you must research this
  thoroughly" — meaning *practice*, not *read again*.
- **3 and 4** are a mandate for lateral study. 3 is about *arts* (adjacent crafts
  whose technique transfers); 4 is about *trades* (how the other parties in your
  system actually work and what they are incentivized to do). Failures cluster at
  the seams between trades because no one knows both sides.
- **5** is unit economics and opportunity cost, stated four hundred years early.
- **6** is the payoff of 2: judgment fast enough to use, which cannot be installed
  by instruction.
- **7** is *kan no me*, perception over sight — reading intent, constraint, and
  second-order effect rather than the visible move.
- **8** is the leading-indicator principle: small details carry the state of the
  whole, and the trifle you ignore is usually the early signal.
- **9** is the cutting edge of the set. Applied honestly it deletes most roadmaps,
  most meetings, and most of any document you are about to write.

## How to use Earth in a build or a critique

**Building**: define the endeavor in one plain sentence; state what winning is in
verifiable terms; inventory the actual timber (people, money, time, assets,
advantages) and assign each to the place its grain fits; name the tools and whether
they're sharp; set the sequence and say what gets done fast and what gets done
carefully.

**Critiquing**: Earth is thin or hollow when the definition is a slogan, when
"winning" can't be checked by a third party, when resources are assumed rather than
possessed, when good people are set to carrying and knotted wood is holding up the
front of the house, or when the plan has no honest account of its own foundation.
The tell is a strategy that would read identically for a different company.
