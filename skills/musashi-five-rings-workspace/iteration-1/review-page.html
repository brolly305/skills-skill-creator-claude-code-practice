Five Rings Scroll: A two-man junk removal outfit in Sacramento

The endeavor: Two men and one truck sell labor and disposal capacity by the load, and currently rent every customer from Google.

Winning looks like: By month 12 — $18,000/month gross, no more than 35% of it traceable to Local Services Ads, at least 8 named accounts that call you without a quote, 60+ Google reviews, and a written cost-per-job number you trust.

地 Earth — the ground

What this is, honestly. You are not in the junk removal business. You are in the business of converting two men's hours and one truck's cubic yards into money, and you are currently buying the right to do that from a landlord who raises rent every quarter and will never stop. The junk is not the constraint. The truck is not the constraint. Demand you own is the constraint.

The timber. At ~27 jobs a month you are running roughly 1.5 jobs a day. Two men and a truck can do three or four. You are not at capacity — you are at about 40% of it. That single fact reframes everything: you do not have a cost problem, you have an idle-truck problem, and rising lead cost only feels fatal because there is nothing else feeding the truck.

Now place the timber:

  • You are a front pillar being used as scaffolding. You are the only person in this company who can build an account relationship, and you spend your week lifting couches. That is the misallocation.
  • Your brother-in-law is a strong inner pillar. Family labor that shows up is not a small thing. But two men on a $150 single-item pickup is two men in the wrong place. A meaningful share of your jobs are one-man jobs.
  • The truck is knotted wood holding up the front of the house. One breakdown is a zero-revenue week. You cannot fix that yet. You can stop pretending it isn't true, and you can know what a rented replacement costs before you need one.
  • The asset you don't count: every customer you've already served. Names, addresses, phone numbers, what they had, what they paid. That list is worth more than this quarter's ad spend and most operators throw it away.
  • What you don't have: a second truck, cash reserve, a salesperson, and — most importantly — any idea what a job actually costs you.

The tools, and whether they're sharp. - The phone. Blunt. An LSA lead goes to three or four providers at once. Whoever answers live books it. If you're under a house with gloves on, you are paying for leads that convert for someone else. - Google Business Profile. Almost certainly dull or unclaimed. This is free ground directly adjacent to the ground you're renting. Fix it in week one. - Pricing. Probably by feel. You need a written price card by load fraction, posted in the truck, that both of you quote from identically. - Your insurance certificate and business license. You think of these as overhead. They are a weapon — they are the exact reason a property manager can hire you and cannot hire the guy with the pickup on Marketplace. - Dump receipts. You have twelve months of them. They contain your real cost structure and you have never added them up.

The layout. Months 1–2: instrument the business and sharpen the free tools. Months 3–6: build the account channel. Months 7–9: raise prices and cut the job types that lose money. Months 10–12: decide the second truck from data, not from a good month. What does not get built at all: a blog, a rebrand, a new website before month 6, and any increase whatsoever in ad spend.

水 Water — the form

The everyday stance. Answer the phone live. Price on site, out loud, before you touch anything. Ask for the review before the truck moves. Log the load. Ten outbound touches every Friday. That is the whole form, and the test of a form is that it survives a bad week — a rainy Tuesday, a sick kid, a truck in the shop. If a routine only runs when you're feeling good, it isn't a routine, it's a mood.

The five techniques to drill. No more than five. You will own these, not consult them.

  1. The live answer. Both phones ring. Whoever's hands are free picks up inside three rings, every time, including on a job. Ask three questions only: what is it, where is it, when do you want it gone.
  2. The walk-and-price. You walk the pile with the customer, you name the price out loud standing next to it, you write it on a card. You never say "I'll text you a number later." The number you text competes on price; the number you say competes on nothing.
  3. The close-out. Before the tailgate goes up: photo of the cleared space, review asked for in person by name, magnet handed over, and one sentence — "next time just text me directly, you don't need to go through Google."
  4. The load log. One line per load: date, job, minutes, tip fee, fuel, what it billed. Thirty seconds in the cab. In ninety days this becomes the most valuable document you own.
  5. The Friday hour. Ten outbound touches before you go home — storage facility managers, property managers, realtors, estate sale companies, small contractors. In person or by phone. Not email.

The training regimen. - Daily: live answer, walk-and-price, close-out, load log. - Weekly (Friday, one hour): ten outbound touches. Read the week's load log. One number: jobs booked, and where each came from. - Monthly (first Sunday, ninety minutes): cost per job, revenue by source, review count. Compare to last month. Write one sentence about what changed. - Quarterly: reread this scroll. Re-price. Decide what to stop doing.

When to drop the form. Musashi's rule is that the mountain and the sea cannot be the same: if a thing fails twice, it will fail a third time, and the change must be to a different kind of thing, not a bigger version of the same thing. Apply it here — if 300 outbound touches over 90 days produce fewer than three booked accounts, the problem is not effort, it is the segment. Change the segment, not the volume. And if a month comes where the phone genuinely does not ring, the answer is more touches and a sharper close-out. It is not more ad budget. Never more ad budget.

火 Fire — the engagement

The opponent. Two, and you have been fighting the wrong one.

The one you think you're fighting is other junk haulers. The one you're actually losing to is the auction itself — a counterparty structurally required to raise your price every quarter until you are exactly indifferent between bidding and quitting. That is not a bug in Local Services Ads. That is the machine working. Think yourself into Google's seat: you are not a customer to be retained, you are an inventory unit whose margin is discovered by raising the price until you flinch. There is no version of the next twelve months where that stops.

The secondary opponent is the national franchises — 1-800-GOT-JUNK, College Hunks, JunkLuggers. From inside their seat: a 7–8% royalty, a national ad fund contribution, W-2 crews, and a pricing floor that makes a $150 single-item job unprofitable for them to accept. They can outbid you in the auction forever. They cannot say yes at 7am for the same day, and they cannot take the small ugly job at all.

The ground. You have been fighting on ground Google chose, on terms Google sets, in a comparison — who pays most per lead — that you lose by design. Ground that favors you: a saved phone number, an account relationship, a same-day yes, and the Sacramento map pack for the neighborhoods you actually service. None of those get more expensive when a franchise raises its bid.

The initiative. Split it deliberately.

  • On Google: tai no sen — receive, do not attack. Stop escalating. Take what comes at a price you've already decided to pay, and let the others bid each other up. Your competitors' commitment costs them more than your patience costs you.
  • Everywhere else: ken no sen — attack first. Nobody is bidding against you for a storage facility manager's phone number. You just have to be the first person to walk in the door and the one they save in their phone.

The tactics.

1. Release four hands (yotsute wo hanasu) — when you and the opponent are locked in the same spirit and neither can progress, abandon that spirit entirely and win by a different method. The move: freeze LSA spend at today's exact dollar figure for twelve months. Write the number on the dash of the truck. Not a cut — a freeze. Every additional dollar of revenue between now and month twelve must come from ground you own. This is the hinge of the entire plan and it is the one you will be most tempted to break in a slow February.

2. Injure the corners (kado ni sawaru) — a strong thing is hard to move head-on, so strike its projecting edges; when the corners fall, the center loses its strength. The move: stop competing for the one-off residential search entirely and take the work that never enters the auction. Self-storage lien cleanouts. Apartment turnovers and move-out debris. Realtor pre-listing clears. Estate and downsizing jobs. Small contractor debris runs. These buyers do not Google "junk removal near me" — they call whoever they already trust, they're repeat by nature, and the franchises' cost structure can't chase them profitably. Twenty-five in-person visits a month, in a clean truck, with a COI and a W-9 in a folder on the seat.

3. Hold down a pillow (makura wo osayuru) — suppress the opponent's move at the syllable before it's born. The move: the future Google search is the enemy action, and the moment of payment is the only moment it can be killed. Every job, without exception: review asked for out loud by name, magnet on the fridge, "text me directly next time." A customer who texts you in eighteen months is a lead Google never got to sell.

The first exchange. Week one, you do three things: claim and fill out the Google Business Profile properly with real job photos; text the last sixty customers a two-line message asking for a review; walk into five self-storage facilities off Power Inn Road and Auburn Boulevard with a folder and a business card. Nothing dramatic happens. That's normal.

What collapse looks like, so you recognize it and press. The first time a property manager calls you for a second job without asking for a quote, that is the collapse beginning. Do not be polite about it. Musashi's whole instruction on collapse is that hesitation at that exact moment gives it back — if you let the collapsing spirit revive, it will not be a collapse. So the same week: ask for the rest of their portfolio, ask who else at their firm handles turnovers, ask to be put on the vendor list in writing. One won account is not a job. It's a door.

This is the school with the enormous curriculum — impressive breadth that exists to be sold rather than used.

A method for the next 12 months

You asked for a method, not tactics. So here's the honest starting point: you don't have a marketing problem. You have an ownership problem and an idle-truck problem, and marketing is where they're showing up.

Let me show you the math I'm assuming, then the method, then where to start Monday.

What I'm assuming (correct these with your real numbers — the method depends on it)

  • Average ticket somewhere around $300–400, so $8k/month is roughly 20–25 jobs.
  • You work ~20–22 days a month. That means the truck is doing about one job a day.
  • LSA is costing you somewhere between $40 and $70 per lead, and you're booking maybe a third to half of them. Call it $120–200 to buy one job — 30–50% of the ticket.
  • Two people are on nearly every job.

If those are close, here's what falls out of them:

Your truck is idle roughly 60% of every working day, and you're paying Google an escalating auction price for the privilege of staying that way. Two guys, one truck, one job a day, and the entire demand pipeline is rented from a platform whose price you don't set and whose competitors keep multiplying.

That's the actual situation. Every quarter LSA gets more expensive because it's an auction and junk removal has near-zero barriers to entry — every new guy with a pickup bids into the same box you're in. There is no version of the next 12 months where you win that auction. The people who win it are the ones with call centers and $2M ad budgets amortized across 40 trucks.

So the method isn't "how do I get cheaper leads." It's how do I move my demand off the auction and onto assets I own, using the resource I actually have in surplus — idle time.

The method: the Constraint Loop

This is the thing to come back to when it goes sideways. It has four parts: a scoreboard, a ladder, a rhythm, and one rule.

1. The scoreboard — five numbers, tracked weekly

One sheet. Paper is fine. Every Friday, 15 minutes.

# Number How to get it
1 Revenue per truck-day Month's revenue ÷ days the truck left the yard
2 Gross profit per truck-day Same, but subtract fuel, dump/disposal fees, and any hired labor first. Do not subtract your own pay — that's the thing you're trying to grow.
3 Booked rate Jobs booked ÷ real inbound opportunities (not spam, not wrong-service)
4 Paid CAC % Ad spend ÷ revenue that came from ads
5 Owned ratio % of the month's revenue from repeat customers, referrals, and commercial accounts — anything that did not come from an auction

Number 5 is the one that decides whether you're building a business or renting one. Right now it's probably under 10%. In 12 months it should be over 40%. That single number is the whole strategy.

2. The ladder — what to fix, in order

At any given moment exactly one thing is limiting you. Check them in this order and stop at the first one that's broken. The order matters: the top of the ladder is free to fix, the bottom costs money.

1. Margin — are the jobs even worth doing? Binding if gross profit per truck-day is under ~$500 or your gross margin is under 50%. This is where most junk removal outfits quietly bleed. You price by volume. You get charged by weight. Concrete, dirt, shingles, wet yard waste, and old tube TVs will destroy a day's profit while filling a quarter of the truck. Fix: a heavy-material surcharge, a published minimum charge, a "we don't take" list, and per-item fees for the stuff with disposal surcharges (mattresses, appliances with refrigerant, e-waste, tires, paint). Also: scrap metal and resellable furniture are found money on jobs you're already doing.

2. Conversion — are you losing jobs you already paid for? Binding if you're booking under ~40% of qualified LSA calls. If you paid $50 for a lead and let it go to voicemail, you burned $50. Every unanswered call is a cash bonfire. Fix: someone answers every call within two rings during working hours, and any missed call gets a callback plus a text within five minutes. On the call, quote on the spot with a range, not "we'll get back to you."

3. Cost of demand — is the auction eating you? Binding if paid CAC exceeds ~15% of the revenue it produces. Fix is not bidding differently. Fix is #4 below. Meanwhile: cap LSA spend at a hard monthly number and treat it as filler, not base load.

4. Demand — not enough work. Binding if truck utilization is under 50% and 1–3 are healthy. This is where you are. And the fix is in the next section.

5. Capacity — can't fit the work. Binding if utilization is over 75% or you're booked out more than a week. Only at this point does a second truck become a question. Not before. A second truck bought to create demand instead of serve it is how two-person outfits go under.

3. The rhythm

  • Friday, 15 minutes: log the five numbers.
  • First Monday of the month, 60–90 minutes: run the ladder. Four questions: What did the numbers do? Which rung is binding? Is the current bet working? What's the one thing next month?
  • Once a quarter, half a day: the quarterly bet. Pick one — exactly one — thing to change, and define in advance what "it worked" looks like as a number.

4. The one rule

One bet at a time. You are two people. You cannot run four initiatives. Pick the rung that's binding, make one bet against it, give it 90 days, measure, then pick again. Everything you're tempted to add on top of the current bet goes on a list you look at next quarter. The list is not a to-do list; it's a holding pen.

The Fire book, "the body of a rock," and your price war

What the Fire book actually is

Go Rin no Sho has five books — Earth, Water, Fire, Wind, Void. Earth lays out what strategy is and the ground you stand on. Water is your form: stance, gaze, the cuts, the training. Fire is what happens on contact — the fight itself, where things move fast, get hot, and can't be rehearsed. Wind surveys rival schools. Void is what's left when method drops away.

Fire is the one people quote, because it's the only one that reads like a playbook. It has three parts:

1. Position, before technique. Musashi opens not with moves but with ground: keep the sun behind you, keep open space on your left and constricted ground on your right, look down on the enemy from slightly higher ground, and drive him toward bad footing so he has no time to look around. The generalizable claim is that most of a fight is decided by where it happens, and choosing the ground is a move you make before the first exchange.

2. The three initiatives (sen)* — he calls this the most important matter in strategy: - Ken no sen, attacking first. Set the terms, force the response. - Tai no sen, receiving. Let them come, appear weak, take them at the point of their commitment. - Tai tai no sen***, meeting the attack at its inception — not first, not second, but inside their move.

3. About twenty-eight named tactics, each a specific way to break a live opponent's balance. "Holding down a pillow" — suppress an action at the syllable before it happens, stop the at of attack. "Crossing at a ford" — pick the moment, commit, get to the other side. "To become the enemy" — think yourself into his position. "Injure the corners" — a strong thing is hard to move head-on, so strike its projecting edges until the whole loses strength. "The mountain-sea change" — if a tactic has failed twice it will fail a third time, so change to a wholly different kind of thing. "To know collapse" — recognize the instant an opponent begins to fall and pursue it immediately, because "if you let the collapsing spirit revive, it will not be a collapse" (Harris).

Two honest notes. The book is terse to the point of ambiguity — Musashi repeatedly ends a teaching with "you must research this thoroughly" where a modern writer would explain, and translations differ enough (Harris, Cleary, Wilson, Tokitsu) that most quotations you see floating around are one man's rendering, sometimes nobody's. And it's a combat text: zero-sum, single opponent, lethal stakes. Several Fire tactics — frightening, throwing into confusion, "passing on" your spirit to make the enemy slack — are frankly psychological operations. The ethical version is usually still the tactically sound one, but the edge is real and worth naming rather than laundering.

"The body of a rock" — iwao no mi (巌の身)

It is the last teaching of the Fire book, and one of the shortest — a spirit like a great rock, immovable and unassailable, so that living things cannot touch it. That's nearly all the text gives you.

Three things it does not mean:

  • Not armour, and not defence. It isn't about absorbing blows. It's a disposition, not a technique.
  • Not tension. It comes from mastery of the Way — from having trained the form until correct action doesn't require deliberation. Bracing yourself is the opposite of it.
  • Not immobility of the body. Musashi's whole method is fluid; the rock is the spirit, the thing underneath the movement that doesn't get moved.

Its position in the book is the point. It sits at the end of Fire, where Fire hands off to Void: the moment method stops being a set of moves you choose and becomes what you simply are. There's a traditional story that Musashi explained it to his lord as being so immovable in spirit that nothing can shake you — treat that as tradition rather than record.

The practical version: the rock is earned downstream of the training, never upstream of it. A man with no form who decides to be immovable is not a rock. He is stubborn, and he loses.

Now — your competitor

Fire has a lot to say here, and the first thing it says is that you are currently fighting in the wrong spirit.

You are locked in "four hands"

Musashi's yotsute wo hanasu — "to release four hands" — is for exactly this: you and the opponent are gripped in the same spirit, neither can progress, and the contest continues in the same mode. His instruction is blunt: abandon that spirit entirely and win by a different method, because grinding on costs you time and damage. He names the same thing again as the mountain-sea change: if a method has failed twice, it will fail a third time — and the change must be to a different kind of thing, not a variation.

Discounting to hold a renewal is the same mode as their 40% price. If you've done it twice and still lost renewals, that's Musashi's signal, and the answer is not a better discount. Concretely, changing the axis means the comparison stops being price-per-unit: multi-year with a price lock, bundled outcomes, a service layer they cannot staff at their price, a migration/onboarding guarantee, contractual risk transfer. Anything that makes the two products not line up unit-to-unit.

"To become the enemy" — think yourself into his position. Musashi's example is a thief barricaded in a house: formidable to everyone outside, but from inside he's a bird in a net, alone against the world. Almost every misjudgment of a rival comes from reading their capability instead of their position.

So sit in their chair. 40% of your price on a near-identical product is one of three situations, and they demand opposite responses:

  1. Funded land-grab. Price is customer acquisition spend, and there's a burn clock. Their customers are the most price-sensitive in the market and will be the cheapest to take back when they raise prices. Correct initiative here is tai no sen — receive, don't chase, and start building the 18-month win-back list now.
  2. Genuinely lower cost structure — thinner support, no compliance burden, offshore build, narrower surface area. Then their price is real and permanent, and you must move, not fight: up-market, or into the segment their cost structure cannot serve.
  3. Loss leader for some other revenue. Then the product isn't the fight at all and you're defending the wrong hill.

You can determine which from outside: funding announcements, job postings, whether their contracts are annual prepaid or monthly, their support SLA, who they've actually won and how long those accounts have lasted. Do this before another renewal cycle. This is kan no me over ken no me — perceive what they're structurally forced to do next, rather than over-reading the visible move they just made.

Injure the corners, not the center

"Anything strong is hard to move head-on, so strike the corners." A 40% price is the center of their position — don't attack it. Attack what they can't afford at that price: the security review, the SLA, the migration help, the compliance work, the integration nobody wants to build, the escalation path. Take the segment whose purchase decision cannot be made on list price, and let them keep the segment that buys on list price alone.

And read your own corners. Which of your accounts are they picking off first? Those are your projecting edges, and they're where you're already losing structure.

Makura wo osayuru: suppress the action at the syllable before it happens. If you're first hearing about the competitor at the renewal conversation, the fight is already on their ground. The leading indicators sit 90–120 days earlier: usage decline, your champion changing jobs, procurement suddenly asking for benchmarks, a new vendor's security questionnaire circulating. Move the engagement there and you're deciding the terms instead of answering a quote.

Here: in the renewal conversation, in your reps.

And the principle that cuts most of this

"Do nothing which is of no use." Some of these accounts should go. If a customer's decision is purely price and they will always be purely price, defending them consumes the discount budget and the sales hours that should be going to the accounts you can actually hold. He won't do the pricing math, and he had no concept of a market with repeated dealings, switching costs, and customers who talk to each other. What the Fire Book actually is

Quick context: Go Rin no Sho was written by Miyamoto Musashi around 1643–45, in a cave called Reigandō outside Kumamoto, in the last two years of his life. He was roughly sixty, claimed some sixty duels undefeated, and was writing down the method of his two-sword school, Niten Ichi-ryū. It's short — you can read the whole thing in two hours. Most people who quote it haven't, which is why the quotes circulating are usually the three or four aphoristic lines and not the method.

The five books are Earth, Water, Fire, Wind, Void. Roughly: Earth is the overview and the carpenter analogy (strategy as a trade you learn like joinery); Water is your own technique — stance, grip, gaze, footwork; Fire is the fight itself; Wind is a critique of other schools and their errors; Void is what's left when technique becomes unconscious.

Fire is called Fire because fire changes size and shape — it can be a candle or a burning province — and Musashi's core claim is that the two are the same discipline. "The spirit of one man is the same as ten thousand." Fire is where he stops talking about how you hold a sword and starts talking about the encounter: terrain, timing, initiative, reading the opponent, and how to break someone.

It's also the most tactical and the most listy part of the book — about two dozen named techniques. The ones worth knowing:

  • Depending on the place (ba no shidai) — take the high ground, keep the sun behind you, drive the opponent toward bad footing. Choose the ground before you choose the move.
  • The three initiatives (mittsu no sen) — ken no sen, attacking first; tai no sen, letting him commit and taking the initiative inside his attack; tai tai no sen, both moving at once. Musashi's point is that initiative is a decision, not a reflex, and there are three legitimate versions of it.
  • Holding down the pillow (makura o osaeru) — the most famous idea in the book. Suppress the opponent's action at the instant of its inception: stop the "at-" before "attack," the "ju-" before "jump." Not blocking the blow — preventing the intention from becoming a blow.
  • Crossing at a ford — pick your crossing point and commit fully; hesitation mid-river is how you drown.
  • Treading down the sword — step into the attack rather than retreating from it, so he never gets a second one off.
  • Knowing collapse (kuzure o shiru) — everything has a rhythm of falling apart, and when you feel the opponent's rhythm break you pursue without pause. Let him recover and you've given the fight back.
  • Becoming the enemy (teki ni naru) — think from inside his position. Musashi's observation is that people systematically overrate opponents; the man barricaded in the house feels cornered, not strong.
  • Releasing four hands — when you and he are locked in the same spirit and nothing is moving, abandon that approach entirely and win by a different technique.
  • Striking at the corners — you can't push over something big head-on; hit an edge, and when the corner goes the whole thing follows.
  • The mountain–sea change — never do the same thing twice. Three times is fatal.
  • Penetrating the depths — sometimes you defeat someone on the surface while his spirit is intact; then it isn't over, and you have to break it at the bottom.
  • Renewal (aratamu) — when a fight becomes tangled and stuck, deliberately change your own spirit and rhythm to get a fresh one.
  • Rat's head, ox's neck — hold the smallest detail and the largest view at once, and switch between them on purpose.

That's the book. It's practical, unmystical, and slightly repetitive: he ends most sections with some version of "you must research this thoroughly," which is his way of saying words can't do the rest.

"The body of a rock"

Iwao no mi — usually rendered "the body of a rock" (Victor Harris's 1974 translation, the one most quotes come from) or "the body of a boulder." It's the final entry in the Fire Book, and it's about three sentences long:

By "the body of a rock" I mean the acquisition of the Way of strategy so that you become as immovable as a rock, so that ten thousand things cannot touch you.

That's essentially all the text gives you. The meaning most people attach to it comes from an anecdote recorded outside Go Rin no Sho: Lord Hosokawa asked Musashi what he meant by the body of a rock. Musashi called in one of his students and ordered him to commit seppuku. The student immediately began to do it. Musashi stopped him and told Hosokawa: that is the body of a rock.

The point is not toughness, and not immobility in the physical sense — Musashi is emphatic elsewhere that the body must never freeze or fix. It's about undivided resolve. A person with no internal argument going on, no gap between decision and action, presents nothing for an opponent to work with. The techniques earlier in the Fire Book all depend on finding a seam: a hesitation, a wobble, a rhythm you can read. The body of a rock is the state of having no seam. Ten thousand things can't touch you because there's no purchase.

The common misreading is "be stubborn" or "be stoic." It isn't either. It's closer to: once you have actually decided, be so completely decided that the decision has no edges.
