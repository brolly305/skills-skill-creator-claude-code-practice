# The Fire book, "the body of a rock," and your price war

## What the Fire book actually is

*Go Rin no Sho* has five books — Earth, Water, Fire, Wind, Void. Earth lays out what strategy is and the ground you stand on. Water is your form: stance, gaze, the cuts, the training. **Fire is what happens on contact** — the fight itself, where things move fast, get hot, and can't be rehearsed. Wind surveys rival schools. Void is what's left when method drops away.

Fire is the one people quote, because it's the only one that reads like a playbook. It has three parts:

**1. Position, before technique.** Musashi opens not with moves but with ground: keep the sun behind you, keep open space on your left and constricted ground on your right, look down on the enemy from slightly higher ground, and drive him toward bad footing so he has no time to look around. The generalizable claim is that most of a fight is decided by *where it happens*, and choosing the ground is a move you make before the first exchange.

**2. The three initiatives (*sen*)** — he calls this the most important matter in strategy:
- ***Ken no sen***, attacking first. Set the terms, force the response.
- ***Tai no sen***, receiving. Let them come, appear weak, take them at the point of their commitment.
- ***Tai tai no sen***, meeting the attack at its inception — not first, not second, but inside their move.

**3. About twenty-eight named tactics**, each a specific way to break a live opponent's balance. "Holding down a pillow" — suppress an action at the syllable before it happens, stop the *at* of *attack*. "Crossing at a ford" — pick the moment, commit, get to the other side. "To become the enemy" — think yourself into his position. "Injure the corners" — a strong thing is hard to move head-on, so strike its projecting edges until the whole loses strength. "The mountain-sea change" — if a tactic has failed twice it will fail a third time, so change to a wholly different kind of thing. "To know collapse" — recognize the instant an opponent begins to fall and pursue it immediately, because "if you let the collapsing spirit revive, it will not be a collapse" (Harris).

Two honest notes. The book is terse to the point of ambiguity — Musashi repeatedly ends a teaching with "you must research this thoroughly" where a modern writer would explain, and translations differ enough (Harris, Cleary, Wilson, Tokitsu) that most quotations you see floating around are one man's rendering, sometimes nobody's. And it's a combat text: zero-sum, single opponent, lethal stakes. Several Fire tactics — frightening, throwing into confusion, "passing on" your spirit to make the enemy slack — are frankly psychological operations. The ethical version is usually still the tactically sound one, but the edge is real and worth naming rather than laundering.

## "The body of a rock" — *iwao no mi* (巌の身)

It is the **last teaching of the Fire book**, and one of the shortest — a spirit like a great rock, immovable and unassailable, so that living things cannot touch it. That's nearly all the text gives you.

Three things it does *not* mean:

- **Not armour, and not defence.** It isn't about absorbing blows. It's a disposition, not a technique.
- **Not tension.** It comes from mastery of the Way — from having trained the form until correct action doesn't require deliberation. Bracing yourself is the opposite of it.
- **Not immobility of the body.** Musashi's whole method is fluid; the rock is the *spirit*, the thing underneath the movement that doesn't get moved.

Its position in the book is the point. It sits at the end of Fire, where Fire hands off to Void: the moment method stops being a set of moves you choose and becomes what you simply are. There's a traditional story that Musashi explained it to his lord as being so immovable in spirit that nothing can shake you — treat that as tradition rather than record.

The practical version: **the rock is earned downstream of the training, never upstream of it.** A man with no form who decides to be immovable is not a rock. He is stubborn, and he loses.

## Now — your competitor

Assumptions, since you didn't specify: a subscription business with recurring renewals, they're newer than you, and you're the higher-priced side. Adjust if wrong.

Fire has a lot to say here, and the first thing it says is that **you are currently fighting in the wrong spirit.**

### You are locked in "four hands"

Musashi's *yotsute wo hanasu* — "to release four hands" — is for exactly this: you and the opponent are gripped in the same spirit, neither can progress, and the contest continues in the same mode. His instruction is blunt: abandon that spirit entirely and win by a different method, because grinding on costs you time and damage. He names the same thing again as the **mountain-sea change**: if a method has failed twice, it will fail a third time — and the change must be to a *different kind of thing*, not a variation.

Discounting to hold a renewal is the same mode as their 40% price. If you've done it twice and still lost renewals, that's Musashi's signal, and the answer is not a better discount. Concretely, changing the axis means the comparison stops being price-per-unit: multi-year with a price lock, bundled outcomes, a service layer they cannot staff at their price, a migration/onboarding guarantee, contractual risk transfer. Anything that makes the two products not line up unit-to-unit.

### Become the enemy before you decide anything

*"To become the enemy"* — think yourself into his position. Musashi's example is a thief barricaded in a house: formidable to everyone outside, but from inside he's a bird in a net, alone against the world. Almost every misjudgment of a rival comes from reading their **capability** instead of their **position**.

So sit in their chair. 40% of your price on a near-identical product is one of three situations, and they demand opposite responses:

1. **Funded land-grab.** Price is customer acquisition spend, and there's a burn clock. Their customers are the most price-sensitive in the market and will be the cheapest to take back when they raise prices. Correct initiative here is *tai no sen* — receive, don't chase, and start building the 18-month win-back list now.
2. **Genuinely lower cost structure** — thinner support, no compliance burden, offshore build, narrower surface area. Then their price is real and permanent, and you must move, not fight: up-market, or into the segment their cost structure cannot serve.
3. **Loss leader** for some other revenue. Then the product isn't the fight at all and you're defending the wrong hill.

You can determine which from outside: funding announcements, job postings, whether their contracts are annual prepaid or monthly, their support SLA, who they've actually won and how long those accounts have lasted. Do this before another renewal cycle. This is *kan no me* over *ken no me* — perceive what they're structurally forced to do next, rather than over-reading the visible move they just made.

### Injure the corners, not the center

"Anything strong is hard to move head-on, so strike the corners." A 40% price is the center of their position — don't attack it. Attack what they can't afford at that price: the security review, the SLA, the migration help, the compliance work, the integration nobody wants to build, the escalation path. Take the segment whose purchase decision *cannot* be made on list price, and let them keep the segment that buys on list price alone.

And read your own corners. Which of your accounts are they picking off first? Those are your projecting edges, and they're where you're already losing structure.

### Hold down the pillow — you're finding out too late

*Makura wo osayuru*: suppress the action at the syllable before it happens. If you're first hearing about the competitor **at the renewal conversation**, the fight is already on their ground. The leading indicators sit 90–120 days earlier: usage decline, your champion changing jobs, procurement suddenly asking for benchmarks, a new vendor's security questionnaire circulating. Move the engagement there and you're deciding the terms instead of answering a quote.

### Where the body of a rock actually applies

Here: in the renewal conversation, in your reps.

A seller who discounts on the first mention of the cheaper competitor has just told the customer your original price was fiction — and once the customer knows that, every renewal forever is a negotiation about how much of your price was made up. Immovability is worth real margin.

But hold to the honest version of *iwao no mi*: **the rock is earned.** If you cannot say, in one sentence, what the customer gets for the extra 60% and have that sentence be true, you don't have a rock — you have stubbornness, and you'll lose slowly instead of quickly. Find out which you have before you instruct anyone to hold the line.

### And the principle that cuts most of this

"Do nothing which is of no use." Some of these accounts should go. If a customer's decision is purely price and they will always be purely price, defending them consumes the discount budget and the sales hours that should be going to the accounts you can actually hold. Losing the right customers to a cheap competitor is a strategy. Losing them by accident is not.

## First moves

1. **This week:** determine which of the three situations they're in. Nothing else should be decided until that's answered.
2. **Before the next renewal cycle:** stop the discount reflex; give reps one true sentence about what the extra 60% buys, plus one non-price concession they're authorized to make (term length, scope, service).
3. **Within the quarter:** change the axis on one deal deliberately — restructure so the two products don't compare unit-to-unit — and see what happens. That's a small mountain-sea change you can run cheaply before you commit the company to it.

## What Musashi won't do for you

He won't do the pricing math, and he had no concept of a market with repeated dealings, switching costs, and customers who talk to each other. His frame is one opponent, one exchange, one of you walks away. Your customers are not enemies, and the competitor may not be the main event at all — in the case where they're burning funding, the real opponent is your own impatience.

What does transfer, and transfers well, is the part you asked about: timing, initiative, ground, and the discipline of reading a rival's position rather than his capability. That's Fire. The rest of the book — Earth's honest definition of what you're actually building, Wind's survey of how everyone else in your market answers this question — is where you'd go next if this turns out to be more than one competitor's opening move.
