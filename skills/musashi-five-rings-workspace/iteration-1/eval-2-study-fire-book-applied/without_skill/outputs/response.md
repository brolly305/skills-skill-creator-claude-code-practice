# The Fire Book, "the body of a rock," and your price war

I'm assuming from "renewals" that you're selling a subscription or contracted product to businesses, with a defined renewal window per account. Everything in the last section rests on that.

---

## What the Fire Book actually is

Quick context: *Go Rin no Sho* was written by Miyamoto Musashi around 1643–45, in a cave called Reigandō outside Kumamoto, in the last two years of his life. He was roughly sixty, claimed some sixty duels undefeated, and was writing down the method of his two-sword school, Niten Ichi-ryū. It's short — you can read the whole thing in two hours. Most people who quote it haven't, which is why the quotes circulating are usually the three or four aphoristic lines and not the method.

The five books are Earth, Water, Fire, Wind, Void. Roughly: Earth is the overview and the carpenter analogy (strategy as a trade you learn like joinery); Water is your own technique — stance, grip, gaze, footwork; **Fire is the fight itself**; Wind is a critique of other schools and their errors; Void is what's left when technique becomes unconscious.

Fire is called Fire because fire changes size and shape — it can be a candle or a burning province — and Musashi's core claim is that the two are the same discipline. "The spirit of one man is the same as ten thousand." Fire is where he stops talking about how you hold a sword and starts talking about the *encounter*: terrain, timing, initiative, reading the opponent, and how to break someone.

It's also the most tactical and the most listy part of the book — about two dozen named techniques. The ones worth knowing:

- **Depending on the place** (*ba no shidai*) — take the high ground, keep the sun behind you, drive the opponent toward bad footing. Choose the ground before you choose the move.
- **The three initiatives** (*mittsu no sen*) — *ken no sen*, attacking first; *tai no sen*, letting him commit and taking the initiative inside his attack; *tai tai no sen*, both moving at once. Musashi's point is that initiative is a decision, not a reflex, and there are three legitimate versions of it.
- **Holding down the pillow** (*makura o osaeru*) — the most famous idea in the book. Suppress the opponent's action at the instant of its inception: stop the "at-" before "attack," the "ju-" before "jump." Not blocking the blow — preventing the intention from becoming a blow.
- **Crossing at a ford** — pick your crossing point and commit fully; hesitation mid-river is how you drown.
- **Treading down the sword** — step *into* the attack rather than retreating from it, so he never gets a second one off.
- **Knowing collapse** (*kuzure o shiru*) — everything has a rhythm of falling apart, and when you feel the opponent's rhythm break you pursue without pause. Let him recover and you've given the fight back.
- **Becoming the enemy** (*teki ni naru*) — think from inside his position. Musashi's observation is that people systematically overrate opponents; the man barricaded in the house feels cornered, not strong.
- **Releasing four hands** — when you and he are locked in the same spirit and nothing is moving, abandon that approach entirely and win by a different technique.
- **Striking at the corners** — you can't push over something big head-on; hit an edge, and when the corner goes the whole thing follows.
- **The mountain–sea change** — never do the same thing twice. Three times is fatal.
- **Penetrating the depths** — sometimes you defeat someone on the surface while his spirit is intact; then it isn't over, and you have to break it at the bottom.
- **Renewal** (*aratamu*) — when a fight becomes tangled and stuck, deliberately change your own spirit and rhythm to get a fresh one.
- **Rat's head, ox's neck** — hold the smallest detail and the largest view at once, and switch between them on purpose.

That's the book. It's practical, unmystical, and slightly repetitive: he ends most sections with some version of "you must research this thoroughly," which is his way of saying words can't do the rest.

## "The body of a rock"

*Iwao no mi* — usually rendered "the body of a rock" (Victor Harris's 1974 translation, the one most quotes come from) or "the body of a boulder." It's the final entry in the Fire Book, and it's about three sentences long:

> By "the body of a rock" I mean the acquisition of the Way of strategy so that you become as immovable as a rock, so that ten thousand things cannot touch you.

That's essentially all the text gives you. The meaning most people attach to it comes from an anecdote recorded outside *Go Rin no Sho*: Lord Hosokawa asked Musashi what he meant by the body of a rock. Musashi called in one of his students and ordered him to commit seppuku. The student immediately began to do it. Musashi stopped him and told Hosokawa: *that* is the body of a rock.

The point is not toughness, and not immobility in the physical sense — Musashi is emphatic elsewhere that the body must never freeze or fix. It's about *undivided resolve*. A person with no internal argument going on, no gap between decision and action, presents nothing for an opponent to work with. The techniques earlier in the Fire Book all depend on finding a seam: a hesitation, a wobble, a rhythm you can read. The body of a rock is the state of having no seam. Ten thousand things can't touch you because there's no purchase.

The common misreading is "be stubborn" or "be stoic." It isn't either. It's closer to: once you have actually decided, be so completely decided that the decision has no edges.

## Is any of this useful for your situation?

Honest answer first: partly, and less than the internet would suggest. *Five Rings* is a duel manual. It's excellent on initiative, timing, terrain, and morale, and it says nothing whatsoever about unit economics, switching costs, or customer psychology — which are the actual mechanics of what's happening to you. Anyone who tells you Musashi has the answer to a SaaS price war is selling something. What it *does* give you is a diagnostic frame, and I think it points at the right things here.

**Become the enemy first.** Before anything else, work out what 40% of your price means for them. There are only a few possibilities and they lead to opposite responses: (a) they're buying market share with someone else's money, in which case the price is a temporary weapon and your job is to survive the window and be there when it lifts; (b) they've genuinely stripped cost out — no support, no compliance work, thin infrastructure, offshore delivery — in which case they aren't selling your product, they're selling a different one that looks like yours in a demo; (c) they're structurally cheaper and it's durable, in which case you have a real strategic problem and price is the least of it. Most companies in your position never actually do this work and just assume (c) out of fear. Musashi's line about the man barricaded in the house is well-taken: from outside, a competitor burning cash at 40% margin-negative pricing looks like an unstoppable force. From inside, it feels like a countdown.

**You are currently in "four hands."** Locked in the same spirit as the opponent — he pushes price, you defend price, nothing moves except your margin. Musashi's instruction is not to push harder. It's to abandon the contested technique and win by a different one. Concretely: stop letting the renewal conversation be a price conversation. Change the axis to total cost including switching and failure risk, or to outcomes you can evidence, or to terms and guarantees, or to scope. Discounting *in response* to their number is the one move that concedes their framing while also costing you money.

**Hold down the pillow.** Their attack happens at a known moment — your renewal window. That is an enormous advantage and most companies waste it by showing up in the last three weeks. Get in front of it: renewal conversations opening 90–120 days out, multi-year terms offered before they're under pressure, usage and integration deepened during the term so the switching decision is already answered before anyone asks it. You're not blocking the blow, you're preventing the intention from forming.

**Depending on the place — choose your ground and concede some.** You cannot defend every account and shouldn't try. There will be a segment where price is essentially the only variable and switching is cheap. Losing those is not a rout, it's a decision, and made deliberately it's cheaper than losing them slowly while paying for the privilege. Fight where the ground favors you: deep integrations, regulated or audited buyers, accounts where downtime or a bad migration costs real money, anywhere procurement asks about your roadmap.

**Strike at the corners.** Symmetrically — go take their edge. The customers they serve worst are their earliest and cheapest ones, and a 40% price implies a support and reliability model that will start visibly failing under load. Be positioned to catch that, and start building the evidence now.

**Mountain–sea change: don't discount twice.** If you've already saved one renewal with a concession, doing it again teaches your entire customer base that your list price is fiction — and it will get back to them, because buyers talk. If you're going to move on price, move once, structurally, and for something in return: longer term, expanded scope, a reference, a case study. Never a straight cut.

**Know collapse — including your own.** Watch for the break in their rhythm: a funding event that doesn't land, support quality slipping, churn from their earliest cohort, a pricing page that quietly moves. When it comes, pursue it hard. But also watch your own: the real danger in a price war isn't losing accounts, it's the internal collapse where sales starts pre-discounting to avoid difficult conversations and the floor drops out over two quarters. That collapse is usually invisible until it's finished.

**And the body of a rock is the actual answer to your question.** Whatever you decide about price — hold, cut once and restructure, or segment and cede ground — the thing that determines whether it works is whether the decision has edges. A company that holds its price but sounds apologetic about it, and folds on a case-by-case basis when a rep pushes, gets the worst of both: the lost revenue *and* the reputation for being negotiable. Pick the line, make sure every person who touches a renewal knows exactly where it is and what they may and may not trade for it, and then be unmovable at it. Not stubborn — decided. Ten thousand things can't touch you because there's nothing loose to grab.

If you want to read it: Victor Harris's translation is the one everyone quotes and it's fine. William Scott Wilson's is more accurate and better prose. Kenji Tokitsu's edition has by far the best historical commentary if you want to know which parts are Musashi and which are legend — including the seppuku anecdote, which may well not have happened.
