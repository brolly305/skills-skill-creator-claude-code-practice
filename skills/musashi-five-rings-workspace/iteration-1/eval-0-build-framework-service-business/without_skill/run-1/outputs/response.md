# A method for the next 12 months

You asked for a method, not tactics. So here's the honest starting point: **you don't have a marketing problem. You have an ownership problem and an idle-truck problem, and marketing is where they're showing up.**

Let me show you the math I'm assuming, then the method, then where to start Monday.

---

## What I'm assuming (correct these with your real numbers — the method depends on it)

- Average ticket somewhere around $300–400, so $8k/month is roughly **20–25 jobs**.
- You work ~20–22 days a month. That means the truck is doing **about one job a day.**
- LSA is costing you somewhere between $40 and $70 per lead, and you're booking maybe a third to half of them. Call it **$120–200 to buy one job** — 30–50% of the ticket.
- Two people are on nearly every job.

If those are close, here's what falls out of them:

**Your truck is idle roughly 60% of every working day, and you're paying Google an escalating auction price for the privilege of staying that way.** Two guys, one truck, one job a day, and the entire demand pipeline is rented from a platform whose price you don't set and whose competitors keep multiplying.

That's the actual situation. Every quarter LSA gets more expensive because it's an auction and junk removal has near-zero barriers to entry — every new guy with a pickup bids into the same box you're in. There is no version of the next 12 months where you win that auction. The people who win it are the ones with call centers and $2M ad budgets amortized across 40 trucks.

So the method isn't "how do I get cheaper leads." It's **how do I move my demand off the auction and onto assets I own, using the resource I actually have in surplus — idle time.**

---

## The method: the Constraint Loop

This is the thing to come back to when it goes sideways. It has four parts: a scoreboard, a ladder, a rhythm, and one rule.

### 1. The scoreboard — five numbers, tracked weekly

One sheet. Paper is fine. Every Friday, 15 minutes.

| # | Number | How to get it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Revenue per truck-day** | Month's revenue ÷ days the truck left the yard |
| 2 | **Gross profit per truck-day** | Same, but subtract fuel, dump/disposal fees, and any hired labor first. Do *not* subtract your own pay — that's the thing you're trying to grow. |
| 3 | **Booked rate** | Jobs booked ÷ real inbound opportunities (not spam, not wrong-service) |
| 4 | **Paid CAC %** | Ad spend ÷ revenue that came from ads |
| 5 | **Owned ratio** | % of the month's revenue from repeat customers, referrals, and commercial accounts — anything that did *not* come from an auction |

Number 5 is the one that decides whether you're building a business or renting one. Right now it's probably under 10%. **In 12 months it should be over 40%.** That single number is the whole strategy.

### 2. The ladder — what to fix, in order

At any given moment exactly one thing is limiting you. Check them in this order and stop at the first one that's broken. The order matters: the top of the ladder is free to fix, the bottom costs money.

**1. Margin — are the jobs even worth doing?**
Binding if gross profit per truck-day is under ~$500 or your gross margin is under 50%.
*This is where most junk removal outfits quietly bleed.* You price by volume. You get charged by weight. Concrete, dirt, shingles, wet yard waste, and old tube TVs will destroy a day's profit while filling a quarter of the truck. Fix: a heavy-material surcharge, a published minimum charge, a "we don't take" list, and per-item fees for the stuff with disposal surcharges (mattresses, appliances with refrigerant, e-waste, tires, paint). Also: scrap metal and resellable furniture are found money on jobs you're already doing.

**2. Conversion — are you losing jobs you already paid for?**
Binding if you're booking under ~40% of qualified LSA calls.
If you paid $50 for a lead and let it go to voicemail, you burned $50. Every unanswered call is a cash bonfire. Fix: someone answers every call within two rings during working hours, and any missed call gets a callback plus a text within five minutes. On the call, quote on the spot with a range, not "we'll get back to you."

**3. Cost of demand — is the auction eating you?**
Binding if paid CAC exceeds ~15% of the revenue it produces.
Fix is *not* bidding differently. Fix is #4 below. Meanwhile: cap LSA spend at a hard monthly number and treat it as filler, not base load.

**4. Demand — not enough work.**
Binding if truck utilization is under 50% and 1–3 are healthy.
This is where you are. And the fix is in the next section.

**5. Capacity — can't fit the work.**
Binding if utilization is over 75% or you're booked out more than a week.
Only at this point does a second truck become a question. Not before. A second truck bought to *create* demand instead of *serve* it is how two-person outfits go under.

### 3. The rhythm

- **Friday, 15 minutes:** log the five numbers.
- **First Monday of the month, 60–90 minutes:** run the ladder. Four questions: What did the numbers do? Which rung is binding? Is the current bet working? What's the one thing next month?
- **Once a quarter, half a day:** the quarterly bet. Pick one — exactly one — thing to change, and define in advance what "it worked" looks like as a number.

### 4. The one rule

**One bet at a time.** You are two people. You cannot run four initiatives. Pick the rung that's binding, make one bet against it, give it 90 days, measure, then pick again. Everything you're tempted to add on top of the current bet goes on a list you look at next quarter. The list is not a to-do list; it's a holding pen.

## Where to start: the demand fix

Your surplus resource is idle afternoons. The highest-return use of an idle afternoon in this business is **building commercial accounts**, because commercial work is repeat, schedulable, and not purchased at auction.

The customer types that generate recurring junk removal in Sacramento:

- **Property managers** — apartment turnovers, evictions, abandoned property. Highest frequency by a mile.
- **Realtors and estate liquidators** — pre-listing cleanouts, probate. Sacramento has heavy estate volume.
- **Self-storage facilities** — abandoned and auctioned units.
- **Senior move managers / probate attorneys**
- **Remodeling and flooring contractors** — demo debris on a schedule
- **Small multifamily landlords, HOAs, mobile home parks**
- **Furniture and appliance retailers** — haul-away of the old unit on delivery

Here's why you can win these and 1-800-GOT-JUNK often can't: **property managers do not buy on price. They buy on reliability, documentation, and invoicing.** They want someone who picks up, shows up when promised, sends before/after photos, and bills them monthly. A two-person outfit where the owner answers the phone is genuinely better at that than a franchise dispatch center. That's not a story you're telling — it's a real structural advantage, and it's the only one you've got. Build on it.

The approach is in-person, not cold email. Idle afternoon, clean truck, clean shirt, drop by the leasing office. Ask one question — "who do you use now for turnover cleanouts, and what goes wrong with them?" — and shut up. What goes wrong is almost always *they don't show up when they say they will.* That's your offer.

**Before you start, check two blockers:** you need general liability insurance (most property managers will ask for a certificate of insurance, often $1M/$2M) and a current business license. If you don't have the COI, you can't close these accounts, full stop. Fix that in week one. Also know going in that commercial work often means net-30 terms, which is a cash-flow gap you need to plan for.

And ask this: **do both of you need to be on every job?** If a meaningful share of your jobs are one-person jobs, then one of you runs the truck while the other develops accounts. That doubles your effective output without a dollar of spend.

## The 12 months, as four gates (not a calendar)

Calendars break the first time something goes sideways. Gates don't. You don't advance until the previous gate clears — however long that takes.

**Gate 1 — Instrument and raise the floor.** *(target: weeks 1–8)*
Numbers tracked weekly. Minimum charge set. Heavy-material surcharge in place. Every call answered. COI in hand.
**Clears when:** gross profit per truck-day is up 25%+ on the same or fewer jobs.

**Gate 2 — First owned channel.** *(target: months 3–6)*
Ten named commercial relationships worked, in person, repeatedly. Not a mailing list — ten names you'd recognize.
**Clears when:** three of them are sending work and the owned ratio hits 25%.

**Gate 3 — Compounding.** *(target: months 6–9)*
Reviews requested on every single job, no exceptions. Google Business Profile actually filled out and posted to. Past customers contacted twice a year. Ad spend capped and now serving as filler between account work.
**Clears when:** owned ratio hits 40% and you're consistently booked 3+ days out.

**Gate 4 — Capacity decision.** *(target: months 9–12)*
Now, and only now, the second truck is a real question — because you have a backlog to justify it. The alternative answer is equally valid: stay one truck, go narrower and higher-value (estate/probate cleanouts, hoarding, commercial-only), and take the margin instead of the volume.
**Clears when:** the decision is made on evidence, not on a good month.

## When it goes sideways

This is the part you asked for. Pre-decide these now, while you're calm.

| What happens | The reflex (wrong) | The move |
|---|---|---|
| **Phone dead 2+ weeks** | Raise ad spend | Run the ladder. If demand is binding, hit the account list — every idle hour goes to in-person visits. Ad spend stays capped. |
| **LSA jumps another 20%** | Match the bid | Do nothing to the bid. This is the thesis confirming itself. Cap spend, accept fewer paid jobs, put the freed hours into Gate 2. |
| **Cash gets tight** | Cut price to win jobs | Never cut price — it lowers margin *and* signals commodity. Instead: cut ad spend first (it's your largest discretionary cost), then chase receivables, then take the low-margin work you'd normally decline — temporarily, and knowingly. |
| **Big month, then a crash** | Assume the good month was the new normal | It wasn't. Judge on trailing 3-month averages only. Never make a capacity or hiring decision off one month. |
| **Truck goes down** | Panic | You have one truck; this *will* happen. Have the answer written down now: who rents you a box truck same-day, what it costs, which shop turns you around fastest, and a reserve that covers 10 days of downtime. Until that reserve exists, it is your #1 financial priority. |
| **A commercial account goes dark** | Assume you did something wrong | Usually staff turnover at their end, not performance. Call the new person within a week. But also: no single account should exceed 25% of revenue. If one does, that's a risk, not a win. |
| **A job way beyond your capacity comes in** | Say yes and figure it out | Quote it with a real number that includes hired day labor and extra dump runs. If the number scares them off, that was the right outcome. |
| **You and your brother-in-law start grinding on each other** | Let it ride | See below. |

## The two things most likely to actually kill this

Neither is marketing.

**One truck.** One breakdown and revenue goes to zero. Build the downtime reserve and know your rental option before you need it.

**Two family partners with undefined roles and undefined pay.** This is the single most common way small crews like yours die, and it almost never gets addressed until it's too late. Before month three, get three things in writing between you: who decides what when you disagree, how money is split (and what happens when one of you works more hours than the other), and what happens if one of you wants out. Do it now, while you like each other. Doing it later, when you don't, is a different and much worse conversation.

The goal at the end of 12 months isn't a bigger number. It's that when Google raises its price again, **it doesn't decide your year.**
