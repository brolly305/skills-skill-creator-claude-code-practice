You start by admitting what the business actually is. Then you stop trying to win the auction and make the auction small.

Here is the method, laid out as a scroll you can come back to when things go sideways. Read the whole thing once. After that, go to the one ring that matches whatever broke.

# Five Rings Scroll: A two-man junk removal outfit in Sacramento

**The endeavor:** Two men and one truck sell labor and disposal capacity by the load, and currently rent every customer from Google.

**Winning looks like:** By month 12 — $18,000/month gross, no more than 35% of it traceable to Local Services Ads, at least 8 named accounts that call you without a quote, 60+ Google reviews, and a written cost-per-job number you trust.

**Written on assumptions** (correct any of these and the numbers move, not the method): average ticket around $300, so ~27 jobs/month; LSA spend somewhere near $1,500–2,000/month; the truck is a single non-redundant asset; you and your brother-in-law both ride on most jobs; nobody is currently tracking cost per job.

---

## 地 Earth — the ground

**What this is, honestly.** You are not in the junk removal business. You are in the business of converting two men's hours and one truck's cubic yards into money, and you are currently buying the right to do that from a landlord who raises rent every quarter and will never stop. The junk is not the constraint. The truck is not the constraint. Demand you own is the constraint.

**The timber.** At ~27 jobs a month you are running roughly 1.5 jobs a day. Two men and a truck can do three or four. **You are not at capacity — you are at about 40% of it.** That single fact reframes everything: you do not have a cost problem, you have an idle-truck problem, and rising lead cost only feels fatal because there is nothing else feeding the truck.

Now place the timber:

- **You** are a front pillar being used as scaffolding. You are the only person in this company who can build an account relationship, and you spend your week lifting couches. That is the misallocation.
- **Your brother-in-law** is a strong inner pillar. Family labor that shows up is not a small thing. But two men on a $150 single-item pickup is two men in the wrong place. A meaningful share of your jobs are one-man jobs.
- **The truck** is knotted wood holding up the front of the house. One breakdown is a zero-revenue week. You cannot fix that yet. You can stop pretending it isn't true, and you can know what a rented replacement costs before you need one.
- **The asset you don't count**: every customer you've already served. Names, addresses, phone numbers, what they had, what they paid. That list is worth more than this quarter's ad spend and most operators throw it away.
- **What you don't have**: a second truck, cash reserve, a salesperson, and — most importantly — any idea what a job actually costs you.

**The tools, and whether they're sharp.**
- *The phone.* Blunt. An LSA lead goes to three or four providers at once. Whoever answers live books it. If you're under a house with gloves on, you are paying for leads that convert for someone else.
- *Google Business Profile.* Almost certainly dull or unclaimed. This is free ground directly adjacent to the ground you're renting. Fix it in week one.
- *Pricing.* Probably by feel. You need a written price card by load fraction, posted in the truck, that both of you quote from identically.
- *Your insurance certificate and business license.* You think of these as overhead. They are a weapon — they are the exact reason a property manager can hire you and cannot hire the guy with the pickup on Marketplace.
- *Dump receipts.* You have twelve months of them. They contain your real cost structure and you have never added them up.

**The layout.** Months 1–2: instrument the business and sharpen the free tools. Months 3–6: build the account channel. Months 7–9: raise prices and cut the job types that lose money. Months 10–12: decide the second truck from data, not from a good month. **What does not get built at all:** a blog, a rebrand, a new website before month 6, and any increase whatsoever in ad spend.

## 水 Water — the form

**The everyday stance.** Answer the phone live. Price on site, out loud, before you touch anything. Ask for the review before the truck moves. Log the load. Ten outbound touches every Friday. That is the whole form, and the test of a form is that it survives a bad week — a rainy Tuesday, a sick kid, a truck in the shop. If a routine only runs when you're feeling good, it isn't a routine, it's a mood.

**The five techniques to drill.** No more than five. You will own these, not consult them.

1. **The live answer.** Both phones ring. Whoever's hands are free picks up inside three rings, every time, including on a job. Ask three questions only: what is it, where is it, when do you want it gone.
2. **The walk-and-price.** You walk the pile with the customer, you name the price out loud standing next to it, you write it on a card. You never say "I'll text you a number later." The number you text competes on price; the number you say competes on nothing.
3. **The close-out.** Before the tailgate goes up: photo of the cleared space, review asked for in person by name, magnet handed over, and one sentence — *"next time just text me directly, you don't need to go through Google."*
4. **The load log.** One line per load: date, job, minutes, tip fee, fuel, what it billed. Thirty seconds in the cab. In ninety days this becomes the most valuable document you own.
5. **The Friday hour.** Ten outbound touches before you go home — storage facility managers, property managers, realtors, estate sale companies, small contractors. In person or by phone. Not email.

**The training regimen.**
- *Daily:* live answer, walk-and-price, close-out, load log.
- *Weekly (Friday, one hour):* ten outbound touches. Read the week's load log. One number: jobs booked, and where each came from.
- *Monthly (first Sunday, ninety minutes):* cost per job, revenue by source, review count. Compare to last month. Write one sentence about what changed.
- *Quarterly:* reread this scroll. Re-price. Decide what to stop doing.

**When to drop the form.** Musashi's rule is that the mountain and the sea cannot be the same: if a thing fails twice, it will fail a third time, and the change must be to a *different kind* of thing, not a bigger version of the same thing. Apply it here — if 300 outbound touches over 90 days produce fewer than three booked accounts, the problem is not effort, it is the segment. Change the segment, not the volume. And if a month comes where the phone genuinely does not ring, the answer is more touches and a sharper close-out. It is not more ad budget. Never more ad budget.

## 火 Fire — the engagement

**The opponent.** Two, and you have been fighting the wrong one.

The one you *think* you're fighting is other junk haulers. The one you're actually losing to is **the auction itself** — a counterparty structurally required to raise your price every quarter until you are exactly indifferent between bidding and quitting. That is not a bug in Local Services Ads. That is the machine working. Think yourself into Google's seat: you are not a customer to be retained, you are an inventory unit whose margin is discovered by raising the price until you flinch. There is no version of the next twelve months where that stops.

The secondary opponent is the national franchises — 1-800-GOT-JUNK, College Hunks, JunkLuggers. From inside *their* seat: a 7–8% royalty, a national ad fund contribution, W-2 crews, and a pricing floor that makes a $150 single-item job unprofitable for them to accept. They can outbid you in the auction forever. They cannot say yes at 7am for the same day, and they cannot take the small ugly job at all.

**The ground.** You have been fighting on ground Google chose, on terms Google sets, in a comparison — who pays most per lead — that you lose by design. Ground that favors you: a saved phone number, an account relationship, a same-day yes, and the Sacramento map pack for the neighborhoods you actually service. None of those get more expensive when a franchise raises its bid.

**The initiative.** Split it deliberately.

- On Google: **tai no sen** — receive, do not attack. Stop escalating. Take what comes at a price you've already decided to pay, and let the others bid each other up. Your competitors' commitment costs them more than your patience costs you.
- Everywhere else: **ken no sen** — attack first. Nobody is bidding against you for a storage facility manager's phone number. You just have to be the first person to walk in the door and the one they save in their phone.

**The tactics.**

**1. Release four hands** (*yotsute wo hanasu*) — when you and the opponent are locked in the same spirit and neither can progress, abandon that spirit entirely and win by a different method. **The move: freeze LSA spend at today's exact dollar figure for twelve months.** Write the number on the dash of the truck. Not a cut — a freeze. Every additional dollar of revenue between now and month twelve must come from ground you own. This is the hinge of the entire plan and it is the one you will be most tempted to break in a slow February.

**2. Injure the corners** (*kado ni sawaru*) — a strong thing is hard to move head-on, so strike its projecting edges; when the corners fall, the center loses its strength. **The move: stop competing for the one-off residential search entirely and take the work that never enters the auction.** Self-storage lien cleanouts. Apartment turnovers and move-out debris. Realtor pre-listing clears. Estate and downsizing jobs. Small contractor debris runs. These buyers do not Google "junk removal near me" — they call whoever they already trust, they're repeat by nature, and the franchises' cost structure can't chase them profitably. Twenty-five in-person visits a month, in a clean truck, with a COI and a W-9 in a folder on the seat.

**3. Hold down a pillow** (*makura wo osayuru*) — suppress the opponent's move at the syllable before it's born. **The move: the future Google search is the enemy action, and the moment of payment is the only moment it can be killed.** Every job, without exception: review asked for out loud by name, magnet on the fridge, "text me directly next time." A customer who texts you in eighteen months is a lead Google never got to sell.

**The first exchange.** Week one, you do three things: claim and fill out the Google Business Profile properly with real job photos; text the last sixty customers a two-line message asking for a review; walk into five self-storage facilities off Power Inn Road and Auburn Boulevard with a folder and a business card. Nothing dramatic happens. That's normal.

**What collapse looks like, so you recognize it and press.** The first time a property manager calls you for a second job *without asking for a quote*, that is the collapse beginning. Do not be polite about it. Musashi's whole instruction on collapse is that hesitation at that exact moment gives it back — if you let the collapsing spirit revive, it will not be a collapse. So the same week: ask for the rest of their portfolio, ask who else at their firm handles turnovers, ask to be put on the vendor list in writing. One won account is not a job. It's a door.

## 風 Wind — the other schools

**The national franchises** (1-800-GOT-JUNK, College Hunks, JunkLuggers). *What they do:* uniformed crews, national call center, a published volume-based price chart, heavy paid search and LSA presence. *What they believe wins:* brand trust plus ad dominance. *Where it fails:* royalty and ad-fund overhead force a price floor; a corporate call center cannot commit to same-day at 7am; they turn down or overprice the small ugly job. *Take:* the written volume price chart, the clean truck and matching shirts, and the call center's discipline of answering every single call live. *Refuse:* the belief that you can win by spending. You cannot out-bid them and you must never try.

**The Marketplace/Craigslist solo hauler.** *What they do:* pickup truck, cash, sixty-dollar loads, no insurance, no license, no receipts. *What they believe wins:* lowest price. *Where it fails:* completely locked out of commercial work — no COI, no W-9, no invoice, and property managers cannot legally use them. *Take:* their speed and their willingness to take the disgusting job everyone else declines. *Refuse:* cash-only informality. It's the very thing that would bar you from the segment you're going after.

**The SEO agency / content-marketing school.** *What they do:* sell you a site rebuild, a blog about how to dispose of a mattress in Sacramento, a backlink package, a monthly retainer. *What they believe wins:* ranking. *Where it fails:* twelve-to-eighteen month payback on money you don't have, and the retainer is charged whether or not it works. *Take:* the Google Business Profile and review-velocity part. That part is free, fast, local, and genuinely load-bearing. *Refuse:* everything else, for now. This is the school with the enormous curriculum — impressive breadth that exists to be sold rather than used.

**What you do that they don't.** Right now: nothing. You buy the same leads on the same platform as everyone else and you compete on being available. That is the most important line in this scroll and I'm not going to soften it. What you *could* do that none of them can: be a licensed, insured, two-man outfit that answers live, quotes on the spot, says yes the same day, prices a $150 job profitably, and becomes the number a property manager saves. The franchise can't do the small job. The solo guy can't be hired by a company. That gap is real, and it's empty, and nobody in Sacramento is standing in it.

## 空 Void — what remains

**Genuinely unknown.** Your real cost per job — fuel, tip fees, labor hours, truck reserve. Your close rate on LSA leads (not cost per lead; cost per *booked job*, which is the only number that matters). Whether Sacramento property managers and storage operators will hire a two-man outfit — nobody knows this, including me. Whether your prices would hold at +15%.

**Only learnable by contact.** The account channel. No further analysis will tell you whether it works, and reading about it is worth exactly nothing. The cheap test is 50 in-person visits over six weeks, tracked on one sheet. That test costs you six Fridays and a tank of gas, and it either produces booked accounts or it doesn't.

**What you're pretending to know.** Two things. First: "LSA keeps getting more expensive" is currently a *feeling*. You may well be right, but until you pull four quarters of cost-per-lead and cost-per-booked-job, you're managing by grievance. Second, and larger: you have framed this as a marketing cost problem when the numbers say it's an idle-truck problem. At 27 jobs a month you are running a truck at roughly 40% utilization and blaming the price of leads. Those are different diseases with different treatments, and you've been taking the wrong medicine.

**First move, and the checkpoint.** This week, before anything else: sit down with twelve months of dump receipts, bank statements, and your LSA reporting, and build one sheet — jobs, revenue, source, tip fee, hours. It will take one evening and it will be unpleasant. Everything above depends on it and none of it is worth doing without it.

Then: **day 30** — is GBP live, are reviews climbing, is the load log actually being filled in? **Day 90** — how many accounts booked from 300 touches? **Then quarterly**, reread this scroll and re-price. When something goes sideways, don't reread the whole thing. Go to the ring that broke: money and capacity is Earth, the daily discipline slipping is Water, a competitor or a bad month is Fire, someone selling you a new tactic is Wind, and a decision you can't reason your way through is Void — meaning stop planning and run a cheap test.

## The Dokkōdō for this work

1. **Do not raise the ad budget.** Not in a slow month. Not for a promotion. Not ever this year.
2. **Answer the phone or lose the job.** There is no third outcome.
3. **Price on the walk, out loud, standing next to the pile.** Never text a number from the driveway.
4. **No truck moves before the review is asked for by name.**
5. **Ten touches every Friday, whether you are slow or slammed.** Especially slammed.
6. **A job with no logged number did not happen.**
7. **Settle pay and roles with your brother-in-law in writing before the first good month, not after the first bad one.**

Number one will hurt in February. That's why it's number one.
